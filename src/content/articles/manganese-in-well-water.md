# Manganese in Well Water: Black Stains and How to Fix Them

By the WellWater HQ team

Manganese in well water causes black or dark brown stains on fixtures, laundry, and ice cubes once it's above the EPA's secondary guideline of 0.05 mg/L. That number is set for cosmetic reasons, not health, but manganese also carries a separate, higher health advisory: the EPA's lifetime health advisory for chronic exposure is 0.3 mg/L, set specifically to guard against neurological effects with long-term use ([EPA, Drinking Water Health Advisory for Manganese](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2014-09/documents/support_cc1_magnese_dwreport_0.pdf), 2026).

Well owners often mix up manganese with iron, since both metals dissolve out of groundwater and both cause staining. The tell is color: iron stains orange-brown, manganese stains black to dark brown or purplish-black, and the two frequently show up together in the same well.

## What causes manganese in well water?

Manganese occurs naturally in rock and soil, particularly in areas with certain sedimentary and metamorphic geology, and dissolves into groundwater the same way iron does as water moves through the aquifer. Wells in the same regions prone to high iron, parts of the Midwest, the Southeast, and areas with peat or organic-rich soils, are also more likely to carry elevated manganese, since the conditions that dissolve one metal often dissolve the other.

Like iron, manganese is often invisible in water as it comes out of the tap and only darkens after contact with air or after building up in pipes, water heaters, and toilet tanks over time.

## Is manganese in well water dangerous to drink?

At the levels most private wells test at, manganese is treated primarily as a cosmetic and taste problem, not a health emergency; it's regulated as a secondary (non-enforceable) standard for exactly that reason. The health picture changes at sustained higher exposure. The EPA's lifetime health advisory of 0.3 mg/L is the number set specifically to protect against neurological effects from long-term chronic exposure (EPA, Drinking Water Health Advisory for Manganese, 2026), and the EPA recommends that infants under six months not be given water, or formula mixed with water, above that same 0.3 mg/L threshold, since infants process manganese differently than older children and adults.

Manganese isn't currently a federally enforceable primary contaminant, so there's no legal limit the way there is for something like nitrate, but a level anywhere near or above 0.3 mg/L is worth treating as a genuine reason to test further and consider point-of-use treatment for infant formula preparation specifically.

| Manganese level | What it typically means |
|---|---|
| Under 0.05 mg/L | EPA's secondary (cosmetic) guideline; minimal staining |
| 0.05 to 0.3 mg/L | Staining likely; below the health advisory threshold |
| Above 0.3 mg/L | Exceeds EPA's lifetime health advisory; treat before infant formula use, consider whole-house treatment |

## How do you test for manganese in well water?

A certified laboratory test is the only reliable way to get a precise manganese reading, since color alone can't distinguish a moderate level from one approaching the 0.3 mg/L health advisory. Test manganese alongside iron, since a lab panel covering both metals costs little more than testing for one, and the two so often occur together that treating for iron alone can leave a manganese problem unaddressed.

## How do you remove manganese from well water?

Manganese is chemically more stubborn to oxidize than iron, which is why a treatment system sized only for iron often underperforms on manganese even when both are present.

- **Oxidizing filter (air, chlorine, or potassium permanganate injection):** the standard whole-house fix; forces dissolved manganese to oxidize into a solid form a filter bed can trap, similar to iron treatment but typically needing a stronger oxidizer or longer contact time.
- **Water softener:** removes only low levels of manganese, generally under 1 to 2 mg/L, using ion-exchange resin, and works best as a secondary step after oxidation rather than a standalone fix at higher levels.
- **Reverse osmosis:** effective at removing manganese at the point of use (typically a kitchen tap), a reasonable option for households only concerned with drinking and cooking water rather than whole-house treatment.

## FAQ

**What does manganese in well water look like?**
Manganese causes black, dark brown, or purplish-black staining on fixtures, laundry, and ice, and can give water a metallic taste at higher concentrations. It's often confused with iron, which stains orange-brown instead.

**How much manganese in well water is dangerous?**
The EPA's lifetime health advisory for chronic exposure is 0.3 mg/L. Below that, manganese is treated as a cosmetic and taste issue rather than a health concern; the separate 0.05 mg/L secondary standard exists specifically to control staining, not to signal danger at that lower number.

**Can I boil manganese out of well water?**
No. Boiling evaporates water without removing dissolved minerals, so manganese concentration actually increases in the remaining water rather than decreasing, the same effect boiling has on nitrate.

**Does a water softener remove manganese?**
A standard softener removes low levels, generally under 1 to 2 mg/L, but manganese oxidizes less readily than iron, so higher concentrations typically need a dedicated oxidizing filter rather than a softener alone.

**Should I stop giving my baby formula made with well water if manganese is high?**
The EPA recommends that water above 0.3 mg/L manganese not be used for infants under six months, including for mixing formula, until the water is tested clear or treated. Talk to a pediatrician if you're unsure about a specific test result.

Bottom line: black staining means manganese, not iron, and the number that actually matters for health is the EPA's 0.3 mg/L lifetime advisory, not the lower 0.05 mg/L cosmetic guideline. Test for both metals together, and treat with an oxidizing filter sized for manganese specifically if levels run high.
