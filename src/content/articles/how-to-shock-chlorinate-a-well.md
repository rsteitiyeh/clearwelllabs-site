By the WellWater HQ team

Shock chlorinating a well means flooding the well and household plumbing with a strong chlorine bleach solution, holding it in the system for at least 12 hours, then flushing it out. It's the standard fix for a positive coliform bacteria test, a new well, a well that's been serviced or repaired, or a well that's been flooded, and it's a job most well owners can do themselves with careful prep and the right bleach volume for their well's depth and casing diameter.

This is not a five-minute task, and getting the safety steps wrong can cause real harm, from electric shock at the wellhead to chlorine gas exposure in an enclosed well pit. Read the safety section below in full before you start.

## Safety warnings before you begin

- **Turn off all electricity to the well pump before you touch anything at the wellhead.** Working near a well pump while it's powered risks electric shock. If you're not experienced with well electrical systems, have a qualified electrician or well contractor handle this step.
- **Never enter a well pit or other enclosed space around the well.** Gases can build up in these spaces and create a suffocation hazard. This work is for outside the pit only.
- **Wear protective goggles or a face shield and gloves when mixing or handling chlorine bleach solution.** Concentrated bleach can injure eyes, irritate skin, and damage clothing and fabric on contact.
- **Work in a well-ventilated area and avoid breathing chlorine vapors** while mixing and pouring the solution.
- **Close the bypass valve on any water softener or reverse osmosis unit before starting.** Full-strength chlorination solution can damage softener resin and RO membranes; disinfect those separately per the manufacturer's instructions.
- **Do not drink, cook with, or bathe in the water until the full disinfection process, including the final flush, is complete**, and boil any water you must use in the meantime.

These precautions follow [CDC guidance for disinfecting wells](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/how-to-disinfect-wells-after-an-emergency.html) (CDC, How to Disinfect Wells After an Emergency, 2025).

## How much bleach do you need?

The amount depends on your well's water depth and casing diameter, calculated to reach a chlorine concentration above 100 mg/L in the well itself. Use only unscented household liquid bleach (5 to 9% concentration); scented or additive bleach can introduce other chemicals into your water system.

| Water depth | 4-inch casing | 6-inch casing | 8-inch casing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 feet | 3 1/4 tbsp | 1/2 cup | 3/4 cup |
| 20 feet | 6 1/2 tbsp | 1 cup | 1 1/2 cups |
| 50 feet | 1 cup | 2 1/2 cups | 3 3/4 cups |
| 100 feet | 2 cups | 5 cups | 7 1/2 cups |

Figures adapted from the [CDC's bleach disinfection table for drilled and driven wells](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/how-to-disinfect-wells-after-an-emergency.html) (CDC, How to Disinfect Wells After an Emergency, 2025). If you don't know your exact water depth or casing size, a well contractor or your original well completion report can confirm both; guessing on this number is where most DIY attempts under-treat the well.

## Step-by-step: how to shock chlorinate a drilled or driven well

1. Turn off electricity to the well pump and clear any debris from around the wellhead.
2. Run water from the outside faucet closest to the well until it runs clear, to flush out any obviously contaminated water before you start.
3. Close the bypass valve on any water softener connected to the system.
4. Using the table above, mix the correct amount of bleach with 3 to 5 gallons of water in a clean 5-gallon bucket.
5. Remove the well's vent cap and pour the bleach solution into the well through a funnel, keeping it away from any electrical connections.
6. Attach a clean hose to the nearest outside faucet and circulate water back into the well for several minutes to mix the solution thoroughly.
7. Rinse the inside of the well casing with a hose or bucket for 5 to 10 minutes to coat the full casing wall.
8. Open every faucet inside the home, one at a time, and run each until you smell a strong chlorine odor, then shut it off. This confirms the solution reached every branch of the plumbing.
9. Let the solution sit in the well and plumbing for a minimum of 12 hours, ideally overnight.
10. After at least 12 hours, run an outside faucet onto a gravel driveway or another area without plants, since chlorinated water kills vegetation, and continue until the chlorine smell is gone.
11. Run all indoor faucets until the chlorine odor disappears from each one.
12. Boil any water you use for drinking or cooking (a rolling boil for one minute) until the well has been retested and confirmed clear.

## When can you drink the water again?

Not until the well has been resampled and the lab confirms no coliform bacteria. Wait at least 7 to 10 days after disinfection before collecting a sample, since testing too soon can be thrown off by residual chlorine still in the system; water sampling cannot proceed until all chlorine has flushed out. The [CDC recommends a follow-up test](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/how-to-disinfect-wells-after-an-emergency.html) two to four weeks after the first clean result, and another at three to four months, to confirm the fix held (CDC, How to Disinfect Wells After an Emergency, 2025).

If a repeat test still shows coliform bacteria after disinfection, the problem is likely a physical defect in the well itself, such as a cracked casing or bad seal, and needs a licensed well contractor rather than a second round of bleach.

## FAQ

**How long does shock chlorination take from start to finish?**
The active work takes 2 to 3 hours; the solution then needs to sit for a minimum of 12 hours before flushing, and you'll wait 7 to 10 days after that before retesting. Plan on a full process spanning about two weeks start to confirmed-clean result.

**Can I shock chlorinate my well myself, or do I need a professional?**
Most well owners can do a routine shock chlorination themselves with the right bleach volume and safety precautions. Hire a licensed well or pump contractor if the well has an unclear casing depth or diameter, if repeated disinfection hasn't fixed a recurring positive test, or if any electrical work near the wellhead is needed.

**Does shock chlorination damage a water softener or well pump?**
It can, if you don't bypass the softener before starting; the high chlorine concentration can damage resin. Well pumps and plumbing generally tolerate a properly executed shock chlorination without issue, since the concentration and contact time are standard practice.

**Why do I need to wait 12 hours before flushing the well?**
Twelve hours gives the chlorine solution time to fully contact and kill bacteria throughout the well and plumbing system. Flushing too early leaves parts of the system under-treated and can produce a false sense that the job is done.

**How often should a well be shock chlorinated?**
Only when there's a reason: a positive coliform test, a new well, after well repairs or pump service, or after flooding. Shock chlorination isn't a routine annual maintenance step on its own; annual water testing is what tells you whether it's needed.

Bottom line: shock chlorination works when you use the right bleach volume for your well's actual depth and casing size, respect the 12-hour contact time, and confirm the fix with a certified retest before drinking the water again. Skipping the safety steps, not the chlorine math, is where most DIY attempts go wrong.
