# Best iron filter for well water: a spec-based shortlist

By the WellWater HQ team

There is no single best iron filter for every well. The right choice depends on how much iron is in your water, whether it is ferrous, ferric, or bacterial iron, and your water's pH. Three technologies cover nearly all residential cases: air injection oxidation, manganese greensand, and solid catalytic media like Filox or Pro-OX.

Iron above 0.3 mg/L trips the EPA's secondary standard for staining, taste, and odor, a guideline rather than a health rule ([EPA, Protect Your Home's Water](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water)). What actually changes your filter choice is the iron's form and concentration: clear-water ferrous iron behaves differently than red-water ferric iron or the slime layer left by iron bacteria, and each one points toward a different media.

## What type of iron do you have, and does it change the filter you need?

Yes. Ferrous iron is clear when drawn and turns rusty only after sitting, ferric iron is already rust-colored at the tap, and bacterial iron produces a reddish-brown slime that clogs media fast. Each needs a different fix: ferrous oxidizes and filters out cleanly, ferric needs mechanical filtration layered on top of oxidation, and bacterial iron usually needs shock chlorination before any filter media will hold up.

Penn State Extension recommends lab testing to confirm both the concentration and the form before buying equipment, since the two factors together decide which media will actually work in your well ([Penn State Extension, Iron and Manganese in Private Water Systems](https://extension.psu.edu/iron-and-manganese-in-private-water-systems)). Iron and manganese show up throughout the country but cluster in certain regions, and the extension office's own data notes they're most common in Pennsylvania's northern and western counties, a reminder that "normal" iron levels vary a lot by geology.

## What's the difference between air injection, greensand, and catalytic media?

Air injection systems draw air into a chamber to oxidize dissolved iron, then trap it on a media bed, with no chemicals involved. Manganese greensand regenerates with potassium permanganate, a chemical you keep on hand and replenish. Solid catalytic media, sold as Filox, Pyrolox, or Pro-OX, oxidizes iron on contact and lasts the longest but costs more upfront.

Air injection handles iron up to roughly 5 ppm and needs source water at pH 8.0 or higher to work as designed. Below that pH, oxidation slows and the system underperforms regardless of brand. Manganese greensand and solid manganese dioxide media, by contrast, perform best in the 6.8 to 7.5 pH band and don't need that alkaline boost. Solid manganese dioxide media runs over 80 percent purity at 120 to 140 pounds per cubic foot, denser than coated greensand, and typically lasts 10 to 15 years before the bed needs refilling, against roughly 5 years for coated greensand media.

## How do you size an iron filter for your well's flow rate and iron level?

Match the control valve's rated service flow to your well's peak GPM, not the tank's gallon capacity alone, then confirm the media bed depth is rated for your lab-tested ppm level. Undersizing the valve is the single most common install mistake on well systems.

A common valve in this category, the Fleck 5600SXT, is rated for up to 20 GPM service flow at a 15 PSI pressure drop on a properly matched tank. The higher-capacity Fleck 2510SXT is rated for roughly 19 GPM continuous and 24 GPM peak. If your well's tested peak flow runs above the valve's rating, expect a pressure drop every time two fixtures run at once, no matter how good the media inside is.

## Do water softeners remove iron?

A standard water softener strips out small amounts of ferrous iron, generally up to about 3 ppm, through the same ion-exchange process that pulls out calcium and magnesium. Past that level the resin fouls and regeneration frequency climbs fast.

If your lab result comes back above 3 ppm, put a dedicated iron filter ahead of the softener rather than asking the softener to do both jobs. That single change is why some well owners report a softener that "used to work" and now doesn't: iron levels crept up and the resin bed is fouled, not failed.

## What should you check on a spec sheet before you buy?

1. Rated service flow in GPM at a stated pressure drop, not just a headline "max flow" number.
2. Minimum and maximum influent pH the media is rated for.
3. Iron and manganese ppm ceiling, checked against your actual lab result, not a marketing range.
4. Backwash water use per cycle, since your well's recovery rate needs to keep up with it.
5. Media replacement interval and what the warranty actually covers in writing.

## FAQ

**Is iron in well water dangerous to drink?**
No, not at the levels most private wells produce. The EPA classifies iron as a secondary contaminant, regulated for taste, staining, and odor rather than health risk, with a guideline limit of 0.3 mg/L ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water)).

**How long does an iron filter last?**
Coated media like manganese greensand typically needs replacement around every 5 years. Solid catalytic media such as Filox or Pro-OX commonly runs 10 to 15 years before the bed needs refilling.

**Can a water softener remove iron by itself?**
Only at low concentrations, roughly up to 3 ppm of clear-water ferrous iron. Above that, the resin fouls and the softener stops working correctly for hardness too, not just iron.

**Is air injection or greensand better for well water?**
Neither is universally better. Air injection needs pH 8.0 or higher and handles iron up to about 5 ppm with no chemicals. Greensand works across a wider iron range but requires potassium permanganate on hand for regeneration.

**Do I need a water test before buying an iron filter?**
Yes. Penn State Extension and most water treatment dealers recommend a lab test to confirm iron form and concentration before you buy, since a filter sized for the wrong ppm range or pH will underperform no matter how good the brand is.

Bottom line: match the technology to your lab result and pH before you compare brands or star ratings. A correctly sized $1,500 air injection system will consistently outperform an oversold $4,000 unit fighting water chemistry it wasn't built for.
