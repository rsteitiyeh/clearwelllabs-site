A well water filtration system is the equipment that strips sediment, iron, manganese, and organic taste and odor out of your water before it reaches your fixtures. Most homes need it sequenced in two or three stages, sized to a peak flow rate of 7 to 15 gallons per minute, at an installed cost of roughly $1,500 to $5,000 depending on what your water test shows.

Filtration is not the same thing as softening or disinfection, and mixing the terms up is where most well owners overspend or under-treat. A filter physically strains or adsorbs particles and compounds. A softener swaps hardness minerals through ion exchange. Disinfection, usually UV light, kills biological organisms. A full well water setup often uses all three, but the filtration piece specifically is what this guide covers.

## What does a well water filtration system actually filter out?

Filtration handles particulate and aesthetic problems: grit, rust-colored sediment, iron and manganese staining, cloudy water, and the taste or smell of chlorine, sulfur, or organic compounds. It is not the primary defense against bacteria or nitrate, which need disinfection or reverse osmosis instead.

Iron above the [EPA's secondary guideline](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals) of 0.3 mg/L is the most common reason well owners install a dedicated filtration stage, since it stains sinks, tubs, and laundry well before it reaches any health threshold (EPA, Secondary Drinking Water Standards, 2026). Manganese follows the same EPA guideline at a lower threshold of 0.05 mg/L, producing a darker, brownish-black stain instead of iron's orange-red (EPA, Secondary Drinking Water Standards, 2026). Sediment (sand, silt, and fine rock particles pulled up with the water) is the other near-universal problem on private wells, since even a properly developed well pulls some fine material, especially right after pumping changes or a dry spell.

## What types of filters make up a well water filtration system?

A filtration system is rarely one filter. It's a sequence, and the order determines whether the whole thing actually works or clogs itself within weeks.

Sediment filtration comes first, using either a spin-down filter (rinseable, good for heavy grit) or a cartridge filter rated by micron size, typically 5 to 20 microns for whole-house use. Iron and manganese filtration comes next, usually an oxidizing media tank or an air-injection system that converts dissolved iron into a solid form the media can trap. Carbon filtration follows for taste, odor, and organic compounds, certified under [NSF/ANSI Standard 42](https://www.nsf.org/consumer-resources/articles/standards-water-treatment-systems) for aesthetic effects and Standard 53 where it's also reducing health-related contaminants like certain pesticides. Reverse osmosis, certified under NSF/ANSI 58, is usually added at a single tap for drinking water rather than whole-house, since the membrane processes water slowly and produces reject water alongside the treated stream.

We've seen well reports come back with iron at four times the EPA's staining threshold and sediment fine enough to pass through a standard 20-micron filter unnoticed until it clouded a whole glass of water. That's the argument for testing before buying: a filter sized for the wrong micron rating or the wrong contaminant does very little.

| Contaminant | Filtration stage | Typical installed cost |
|---|---|---|
| Sediment / grit | Spin-down or cartridge filter (5 to 20 micron) | $200 to $600 |
| Iron and manganese | Oxidizing or air-injection filter | $1,500 to $4,000 |
| Chlorine taste, odor, organics | Whole-house carbon filter | $300 to $1,200 |
| Nitrate, arsenic, dissolved solids | Point-of-use reverse osmosis | $150 to $600 |
| Nitrate, arsenic, dissolved solids (whole house) | Whole-house reverse osmosis | $4,800 to $8,000 |

## How do you size a well water filtration system?

Sizing runs on two numbers: peak flow rate and contaminant load. A two-bathroom home typically needs 7 to 10 GPM at peak demand, while a four-bathroom home can require 12 to 15 GPM or more, and undersized filtration drops your water pressure the moment two fixtures run at once. Small 5 GPM units cost roughly $400 to $1,200; larger 15 GPM systems built for bigger households run $2,200 to $5,800.

Micron rating matters just as much as flow rate. A 20-micron sediment filter clears visible grit but lets fine silt through; a 5-micron filter catches more but clogs faster and needs more frequent changes. Match the rating to what your test shows rather than defaulting to whatever a big-box store stocks.

## What does a well water filtration system cost, all in?

Most well owners with typical water quality (some sediment, some iron, no bacteria) spend $1,500 to $5,000 on a complete filtration setup including equipment and professional installation, per [HomeGuide's 2026 cost data](https://homeguide.com/costs/well-water-filtration-system-cost) (HomeGuide, Well Water Filtration System Cost, 2026). That range sits above what a city-water household pays for comparable filtration, since well water needs to be treated for particulates and metals a municipal plant already removes before delivery.

Whole-house reverse osmosis is the outlier on cost, running $4,800 to $8,000 installed according to [2026 pricing from This Old House](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/plumbing/reverse-osmosis-system-cost), which is why most homeowners install it at a single kitchen tap instead, where it runs closer to $150 to $600. Ongoing costs are the part people underbudget: expect $100 to $300 a year in filter cartridges and media across a multi-stage system (HomeGuide, 2026), plus more if your well runs unusually high sediment or iron.

## How is a well water filtration system installed?

Installation follows the treatment line in a specific order: sediment first, to protect everything downstream, then iron and manganese removal, then carbon, with each stage tied in after the pressure tank and before the water heater. Installing sediment filtration after an iron filter, or carbon before iron removal, shortens the life of the more expensive components and is one of the most common installer mistakes on retrofit jobs.

A licensed installer typically completes a full multi-stage filtration setup in a single day. Bypass valves on each stage let you swap a cartridge or service one unit without shutting off water to the house, which matters more than it sounds like the first time a filter needs an emergency change on a weekend.

## How much maintenance does a well water filtration system need?

Sediment filters need changing every one to three months, more often if your well runs heavy grit after storms or seasonal water table shifts. Iron filter media typically lasts several years before needing replacement, though the exact interval depends on your iron concentration and daily usage. Carbon cartridges generally need swapping every six to twelve months. Reverse osmosis membranes last two to three years, with the smaller pre-filters needing attention twice as often.

A filtration system does not replace annual water testing. The [EPA recommends every private well owner test at least once a year](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) for total coliform bacteria and nitrate, and every three to five years for a broader chemical panel covering metals and other contaminants (EPA, Protect Your Home's Water, 2026). More than 23 million households in the US rely on private wells for drinking water, and none of that testing happens automatically the way it does on a municipal system ([EPA, Private Drinking Water Wells](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells), 2026). Filtration handles what you already know is in your water; it does not tell you what's new.

Get your water tested by a certified lab before buying anything, not after. A filter chosen from a lab report fits the actual problem. A filter chosen from a store shelf fits whatever the manufacturer assumed your water looked like, which is a different thing.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a well water filtration system, exactly?**
It's the equipment, usually two or three stages in sequence, that removes sediment, iron, manganese, and taste or odor compounds from well water before it reaches your fixtures. It's distinct from softening (which targets hardness) and disinfection (which targets bacteria and viruses).

**What size filtration system do I need for my house?**
Size to your peak flow rate: a two-bathroom home typically needs 7 to 10 GPM, a four-bathroom home 12 to 15 GPM or more. Undersizing causes pressure drops when multiple fixtures run at once; oversizing means paying for capacity you won't use.

**How often do well water filters need to be changed?**
Sediment cartridges typically last one to three months, carbon filters six to twelve months, and reverse osmosis membranes two to three years. The exact interval depends on how much sediment, iron, or organic load your specific well produces.

**Does a filtration system remove bacteria from well water?**
Generally no. Standard sediment, iron, and carbon filtration are not certified for microbiological removal. Bacteria and viruses require UV disinfection or, for some contaminants, reverse osmosis. If your test shows coliform bacteria, filtration alone is not the fix.

**Do I need a filtration system if my well water looks clear?**
Clarity is not the same as safety. Nitrate, arsenic, and bacteria can be present in water that looks and tastes fine. Rely on a certified lab test rather than appearance, and retest annually even after installing a filtration system.
