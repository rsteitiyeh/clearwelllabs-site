Well water filtration system: how to build the right one

A well water filtration system is a chain of treatment stages, usually sediment, iron and sulfur removal, softening, carbon, and UV disinfection, installed after the pressure tank. The right combination depends entirely on a lab water test. Most homes need two or three stages, not all seven, and installed costs run from about $300 to $8,000.

Why does a well filtration system need multiple stages?

City water arrives pretreated. Well water arrives exactly as the aquifer made it, which means you are the treatment plant. No single filter handles grit, dissolved iron, hardness, bacteria, and taste all at once, because each problem needs different chemistry.

That is why a proper well filtration system is built as a train. Each stage does one job and protects the stage behind it. Sediment filters keep grit out of the iron filter. Iron removal keeps rust from fouling the softener resin. Carbon polishes taste. UV disinfects water that is already clear enough for the light to pass through.

We have seen homeowners buy a single cartridge filter and expect it to fix orange staining, rotten egg smell, and cloudy water together. It never does. The good news runs the other way too: if your water test comes back with only sediment and a little hardness, you do not need a $6,000 train. Two stages and you are done.

Start with a water test, not a catalog

Every sizing and selection decision downstream comes from the test. The EPA and CDC recommend testing private wells at least annually for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH (https://www.epa.gov/privatewells and https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/private-wells.html). Roughly 23 million households in the US rely on private wells, and the owner, not a utility, is responsible for water safety.

Use a state-certified lab, not just a test strip kit. Strips are fine for quick checks between lab tests, but treatment decisions need real numbers: iron in mg/L, hardness in grains per gallon, pH, manganese, and a coliform result. If anything on the health side looks off, contact your local health department before you buy equipment. They will tell you what matters in your county's geology.

A basic lab panel typically runs $30 to $150 depending on how many contaminants you include. Compared to buying the wrong $2,000 iron filter, it is the cheapest part of the whole project. The full anatomy of a well water filtration system

Here is the complete train in the order water should flow through it. Almost nobody needs every stage. Think of this as the full menu, then let your test results pick the courses.

Stage 1: pressure tank (not a filter, but it comes first)

Water leaves the well pump and enters the pressure tank, which smooths out pump cycles and holds pressure for the house. All filtration equipment installs after the tank so the filters see steady pressure instead of pump surges. If your tank is waterlogged or short cycling, fix that before adding treatment; we cover the symptoms in our pressure tank guide at WellWater HQ/well-water-pressure-tank.

Stage 2: sediment filter

The workhorse. A spin-down or cartridge sediment filter catches sand, silt, and rust flakes from 5 to 100 microns. Every well system should have one, even if the water looks clear, because grit destroys everything downstream. Cartridge systems cost about $50 to $300 in parts; a self-flushing spin-down unit runs more but skips the monthly cartridge swap. We compare the common designs at WellWater HQ/best-sediment-filter-well-water.

Stage 3: iron, manganese, and sulfur treatment

If your test shows iron above about 0.3 mg/L, you will see orange staining on fixtures and laundry. Above 1 to 2 mg/L, a dedicated iron filter earns its keep. Air injection oxidizing filters handle most residential iron and the rotten egg smell of hydrogen sulfide in one tank, typically $1,200 to $2,500 installed. Higher iron loads or bacteria-bound iron may need chlorine injection ahead of the filter. Our head-to-head of the common approaches lives at WellWater HQ/best-iron-filter-well-water.

Stage 4: water softener

Hardness above about 7 grains per gallon starts scaling water heaters and spotting dishes. A softener swaps calcium and magnesium for sodium on resin beads. It must sit after iron removal, because iron fouls resin permanently. Expect roughly $800 to $2,500 installed for a standard metered unit. Sizing details, salt use, and iron limits are in our softener article at WellWater HQ/well-water-softener-system.

Stage 5: carbon filtration

Activated carbon removes chlorine (if you inject it), tastes, odors, and some organic compounds. On a well it usually rides as a whole-house tank or a large cartridge after the softener. Parts cost is modest, about $100 to $800, and it makes a bigger difference in daily drinking experience than any other stage.

Stage 6: UV disinfection

A UV lamp inactivates bacteria and viruses without chemicals. It only works on clear water, so it always goes last in the whole-house train, after sediment and iron are handled. Budget $400 to $1,200 installed, plus an annual lamp at $60 to $120. If your lab test found coliform, talk to your health department first; shock chlorination of the well itself may also be needed, and that is a diagnosis we will not make from an article.

Stage 7: reverse osmosis at the kitchen tap (optional)

RO strips dissolved solids like nitrates, arsenic, and sodium that carbon cannot touch. Whole-house RO is rarely worth it for a home; a $250 to $600 under-sink unit at the kitchen tap covers drinking and cooking water. Treat it as a polishing stage for the one tap you drink from, not a substitute for the whole-house stages.

How do you match the system to your water test results?

Lay the lab report next to this list and build only what the numbers demand. Here is the shorthand we use when a neighbor hands us their results.

  - Coliform positive: call your local health department, then shock chlorinate and retest. If it recurs, add UV. Do not skip the retest.

  - Iron over 0.3 mg/L or visible staining: air injection iron filter. Over 5 to 7 mg/L, ask installers about chlorine injection instead.

  - Hardness over 7 gpg: softener, sized to household use. Under 7 gpg, most families skip it.

  - Nitrates above 10 mg/L: RO at the drinking tap at minimum, and find the source. Nitrates are a health item, so loop in the health department.

  - Low pH (under 6.5): acid neutralizer tank before everything else metallic, or your copper pipes slowly dissolve.

  - Odor or off taste with clean numbers: carbon stage, and check the water heater anode, a common sulfur smell source people miss.

Notice what is missing from most real-world results: the full seven-stage train. A common central Wisconsin well, for example, needs sediment, iron, and a softener. A common New England well needs sediment, a neutralizer, and UV. Geology decides.

Is a whole house water filtration system for well water worth it?

Whole-house treatment (point of entry) protects plumbing, appliances, and every tap. Point-of-use treatment (like under-sink RO) protects one faucet cheaply. Most well owners end up with both: a whole-house train for the water that touches everything, plus RO where they drink.

The economics usually pencil out through appliances. A water heater scaled by hard water or stained by iron dies years early, and replacing it costs as much as a softener. Fixtures, laundry, and dishwashers tell the same story. If your test shows iron or hardness in the ranges above, whole-house treatment is not a luxury purchase; it is deferred maintenance you are choosing to pay now or later.

Installation, upkeep, and what it really costs to own

A handy owner can install cartridge sediment filters and an under-sink RO in an afternoon. Backwashing tanks (iron filters, softeners, neutralizers) involve drains, bypass valves, and sometimes electrical, so most people hire those out. Plumber install labor commonly adds $300 to $800 per tank, and quotes vary a lot by county; get three.

Ongoing costs are the part sales brochures skip. Sediment cartridges run $5 to $20 each and change every 1 to 6 months. Softener salt is $5 to $8 a bag, one or two bags a month for a family. UV lamps are annual. RO membranes last 2 to 5 years. Add it up and a full train costs roughly $150 to $400 a year to feed, approximate, which is still cheaper than bottled water for a household by a wide margin.

Why stage order matters more than brand

Two identical sets of equipment can behave completely differently depending on plumbing order. The rule that governs everything: each stage must receive water it can actually handle. UV needs clear water. Softener resin needs iron-free water. Iron media needs grit-free water. Reverse the order anywhere and the upstream problem eats the downstream stage.

The most expensive version of this mistake we hear about is UV installed right after the pressure tank on turbid water. The lamp runs, the homeowner relaxes, and bacteria ride through in the shadow of suspended particles. UV manufacturers publish clarity requirements for a reason. Sediment and iron first, always, then disinfect.

Brand matters far less than people think. Most residential iron filters use the same handful of control valve platforms and media types under different paint. When we priced systems for our own homes, the spread between big-name and value brands on identical specs was about 20 to 30 percent, while a wrong stage order made a 100 percent difference in whether the water actually came out clean.

One more ordering note: if you inject chlorine for iron or bacteria, the carbon stage must come after the contact tank so it strips the residual chlorine before the softener and your taps. That single detail decides whether your softener resin lasts five years or fifteen.

Seasonal wells, cabins, and small systems

Not every well feeds a full-time family home. A hunting cabin or seasonal cottage changes the math. Backwashing tanks dislike sitting idle for months; softener resin can foul and iron media beds can channel. For a place used a few weekends a month, we usually suggest a cartridge-based well filtration system: sediment plus a carbon block, with UV if bacteria has ever appeared, and RO or boiling for drinking water.

Winterizing matters too. Cartridge housings crack when they freeze, and a cracked housing floods whatever room it lives in. Drain filters and bypass the treatment train before hard freezes if the building is unheated. It takes ten minutes in the fall and saves a February phone call nobody enjoys.

Small systems also shine as a first step. Plenty of readers install a $150 sediment and carbon setup while they save for the full train, and honestly, if the lab report is mild, some never need more than that.

Mistakes we see over and over

Buying before testing tops the list. Second is wrong stage order, usually a softener installed ahead of iron removal, which quietly ruins the resin. Third is undersized equipment: a 7 gpm iron filter on a three-bathroom house will let iron slip through every time two showers run at once.

Fourth is neglect. A sediment cartridge left in for a year becomes a bacteria hotel and a pressure restriction. If pressure drops at the shower, check the filter before blaming the pump; renters and new owners miss this constantly. A shower-specific symptom rundown is at WellWater HQ/shower-filter-well-water.

Last one: treating symptoms twice. If you fix iron at the point of entry, you do not also need iron-rated shower filters and faucet filters. One good whole-house stage beats five gadgets.

Common questions

What is the best well water filtration system overall?

There is no single best system, because wells differ. The best system is the shortest train that fixes what your lab test found. For many homes that is a sediment filter plus one problem-specific stage, such as an iron filter or softener, with UV added when bacteria has ever shown up. Match stages to numbers, not to marketing.

How much does a complete well water filtration system cost?

Approximate installed ranges: sediment only, $300 to $900. Sediment plus carbon, $800 to $2,000. An iron package, $1,500 to $3,200. A full train with softener and UV, $3,500 to $8,000. Under-sink RO adds $250 to $600. Annual upkeep runs roughly $150 to $400. Quotes vary a lot by county; get three.

Do I need a filtration system if my well water tastes fine?

Taste is not a safety test. Coliform bacteria, nitrates, and arsenic are all tasteless, which is why the EPA and CDC recommend annual testing of private wells. Test first through a state-certified lab. If everything comes back clean, a simple sediment filter may be all you need, and that is a fine outcome.

Can one filter handle iron, hardness, and bacteria together?

No. Iron needs oxidation media, hardness needs ion-exchange resin, and bacteria needs UV or chlorination. Combo units exist that stack two jobs in one tank, and some work acceptably at low contaminant levels, but they trade capacity for convenience. At moderate to high levels, dedicated stages in the correct order outperform any all-in-one.

How often should stages be serviced?

Sediment cartridges: every 1 to 6 months, by pressure drop. Softener salt: check monthly. Iron filter media: 5 to 10 years depending on load. Carbon media or cartridges: 6 months to 2 years. UV lamp: yearly, even if it still glows, because output fades. RO membrane: 2 to 5 years with prefilters changed annually.

Bottom line

Test with a state-certified lab, then build the shortest treatment train that fixes what the numbers show, in this order: sediment, iron and sulfur, softener, carbon, UV, with RO at the kitchen tap if dissolved solids demand it. Most homes land at two or three stages and $1,500 to $4,000 installed, approximate. Retest every year, because aquifers change and so should your system.
