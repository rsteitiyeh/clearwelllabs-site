A well water softener system removes calcium and magnesium through ion exchange, trading hardness minerals for sodium as water passes through a resin bed. Size it by multiplying your hardness in grains per gallon by daily household water use, then match that to a grain capacity that regenerates about once a week.

We install and size these systems for rural homeowners every week, and the same two mistakes keep showing up: buying a softener that is too small for the actual hardness, and putting a softener on iron heavy water without treating the iron first. This guide walks through both, with the math.

Why does well water run so hard?

City water usually gets some treatment before it reaches your tap. Well water comes straight out of the ground, and the ground is where hardness comes from. As rain soaks through soil and limestone, it dissolves calcium and magnesium. By the time that water reaches your aquifer, it can carry a heavy mineral load.

Municipal supplies in the US often land between 3 and 10 grains per gallon. Private wells in limestone country routinely test at 15, 20, even 30 gpg or more. We sized a system last spring for a family outside a limestone ridge whose well tested at 26 gpg. Their previous softener, a small unit rated for city water, had given up years earlier.

Hard water is not a health hazard, but it is expensive. Scale coats water heater elements, clogs fixtures, shortens appliance life, and leaves that chalky film on glassware. The EPA and CDC recommend testing private wells at least annually for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH, and a hardness number usually comes with the same lab panel. See https://www.epa.gov/privatewells and https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/private-wells.html for the testing guidance.

If you have not tested recently, start there. Every sizing decision below depends on a real hardness number, not a guess.

How do you size a water softener for well water?

The math is simple once you have your test result. Here is the formula we use:

  - Daily softening load = hardness (gpg) x number of people x 75 gallons per person per day

  - Weekly load = daily load x 7

  - Pick a grain capacity at or above the weekly load, so the unit regenerates about once a week

Worked example. Say your well tests at 15 gpg and four people live in the house. That is 15 x 4 x 75, which comes to 4,500 grains per day. Over a week that is 31,500 grains. A 32,000 grain softener covers it, though we would lean toward a 40,000 grain unit for headroom, since softeners run most efficiently when they are not pushed to their rated limit every cycle.

One wrinkle worth knowing: if your water carries dissolved iron, add roughly 4 gpg to your hardness number for every 1 ppm of iron before you run the math. Iron eats softener capacity fast.

Quick sizing table

This table assumes 75 gallons per person per day and weekly regeneration. Round up when you land between sizes.

|                    |              |              |              |               |
| ------------------ | ------------ | ------------ | ------------ | ------------- |
| **Household size** | **10 gpg**   | **15 gpg**   | **20 gpg**   | **30 gpg**    |
| 1-2 people         | 24,000 grain | 24,000 grain | 32,000 grain | 40,000 grain  |
| 3-4 people         | 32,000 grain | 32,000 grain | 48,000 grain | 64,000 grain  |
| 5-6 people         | 32,000 grain | 48,000 grain | 64,000 grain | 80,000 grain  |
| 7+ people          | 48,000 grain | 64,000 grain | 80,000 grain | 96,000+ grain |

These are approximate. A metered, demand initiated softener adjusts regeneration to actual use, which saves salt and water compared to the old timer based units.

Salt vs salt free: what actually softens water?

This is where marketing gets slippery, so we will be blunt. Only a salt based ion exchange softener removes hardness minerals from your water. A salt free system, usually template assisted crystallization, is a conditioner. It changes how minerals behave so they are less likely to stick as scale, but the calcium and magnesium stay in the water.

That does not make salt free units useless. On moderately hard water, say under 10 gpg, a conditioner can cut scale buildup meaningfully with no salt bags, no drain line, and almost no maintenance. But if your well runs 15 gpg or higher, or you want soft water feel, spotless glassware, and full scale protection for the water heater, salt based is the honest answer.

| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| **Factor**                 | **Salt based softener**             | **Salt free conditioner**  |

What is the best water softener for well water?

People ask us this constantly, and the honest answer is that the best water softener for well water is the one sized to your actual test results, not a specific brand. That said, well water puts demands on a softener that city water does not, so here is what we would shortlist by spec:

  - Metered, demand initiated regeneration, not a clock timer

  - Grain capacity from the sizing table above, with one size of headroom

  - Fine mesh resin if there is any dissolved iron at all

  - A bypass valve and a 1 inch valve body for decent flow

  - A sediment prefilter ahead of the unit to protect the resin

Handle iron before the softener, not after

Softener resin can technically grab small amounts of dissolved ferrous iron, up to maybe 1 ppm if you use fine mesh resin and an iron cleaning additive. Beyond that, iron fouls the resin bed, and a fouled bed stops softening. Replacement resin is not cheap, and neither is the labor.

If your test shows more than about 1 ppm of iron, or you see red staining in toilets and tubs, put a dedicated iron filter upstream of the softener. Air injection oxidation and manganese greensand systems both work well ahead of a softener. We cover the technologies, and which iron types they handle, in our iron filter comparison at WellWater HQ/best-iron-filter-well-water.

Sulfur smell is a related problem. Some iron filters knock out moderate hydrogen sulfide too, which can save you a separate piece of equipment. Test first so you know what you are treating.

Where a softener fits in a whole house setup

A softener handles hardness and nothing else. It does not remove bacteria, nitrates, sediment, or most tastes and odors. On many wells the softener is one stage in a treatment train: sediment filter first, iron or sulfur treatment next, softener after that, and sometimes UV or reverse osmosis downstream for drinking water.

Operating costs and upkeep

A salt based softener is not a set and forget appliance, but it is close. Expect to buy 40 pound bags of salt, roughly one to two per month for a family of four on moderately hard water. That is somewhere around $10 to $25 a month at 2026 prices, approximate and regional.

Every few months, check the brine tank for salt bridges, a hard crust that keeps salt from dissolving. Break it up with a broom handle. Once a year, clean the brine tank and run a resin cleaner if you have any iron. Resin itself lasts 10 to 15 years on clean water, less if iron sneaks through.

Water use matters too. Each regeneration sends 25 to 65 gallons of brine to the drain. A metered valve keeps that to once a week or so. If your septic system is marginal, mention the softener discharge to your septic contractor; opinions differ on whether brine harms septic bacteria, and some counties have rules about it.

Common questions

Can I use a water softener if my well has iron?

Yes, but only up to about 1 ppm of dissolved iron, and you should use fine mesh resin plus a resin cleaner. Above 1 ppm, or if you see red staining, install a dedicated iron filter upstream. Iron fouls softener resin and shortens its life, so treating iron first protects the bigger investment.

How much does a well water softener system cost installed?

Roughly $1,200 to $3,000 installed for most homes at 2026 prices, approximate. The unit itself runs $600 to $1,800 depending on capacity and valve quality, and labor covers the rest. Add $500 to $1,500 if you also need an iron filter upstream. Quotes vary a lot by county; get three.

Do salt free softeners work on well water?

They condition rather than soften. Salt free units reduce scale sticking but leave calcium and magnesium in the water, so you will not get soft water feel or full appliance protection. On wells above roughly 10 gpg, or with any meaningful iron, we recommend salt based ion exchange instead.

What size softener do I need for 20 gpg hardness?

For a family of four at 20 gpg: 20 x 4 x 75 equals 6,000 grains per day, or 42,000 per week. A 48,000 grain softener covers that with weekly regeneration and a little headroom. Add capacity if your water also carries dissolved iron, about 4 gpg per 1 ppm of iron.

Bottom line

Test your water first, then size with the formula: hardness in gpg times people times 75 gallons, times seven days. Buy one size up from that number, treat iron upstream if it exceeds 1 ppm, and skip salt free units on genuinely hard wells. A correctly sized salt based softener should give you 15 to 20 years of soft water for the price of a monthly bag or two of salt.
