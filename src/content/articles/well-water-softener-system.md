# Well water softener system: sizing, salt vs salt-free

By the WellWater HQ team

A well water softener is sized to your water's hardness in grains per gallon, your household's daily water use, and your iron level, not just the number of people in the house. Salt-based systems actually remove hardness through ion exchange and handle any hardness level; salt-free conditioners only prevent scale buildup and lose reliability above roughly 10 to 15 grains per gallon.

Well water often carries iron alongside hardness, and iron changes the sizing math. A softener sized only for hardness, with iron ignored, fouls its resin bed faster and needs more frequent regeneration than the spec sheet implies.

## How do you size a water softener for well water?

Start with the standard formula: number of people times 75 gallons of daily use times water hardness in grains per gallon gives your daily grain requirement; multiply that by 7, since most softeners regenerate roughly once a week, to get the minimum grain capacity you need.

Here's that math worked through for a real household: 4 people at 75 gallons each is 300 gallons a day. At 15 grains per gallon hardness, that's 4,500 grains of daily load. Multiply by 7 days and you land at 31,500 grains minimum, before accounting for iron. Most small households land in the 16,000 to 32,000 grain range; larger households or harder water can push requirements up to 100,000 grains.

## How does iron change the sizing calculation?

Iron adds to the load a softener's resin has to handle, since the same ion-exchange process that removes calcium and magnesium also pulls out low levels of ferrous iron. The standard adjustment is to add 5 grains per gallon to your effective hardness number for every 1 ppm of iron present.

Continuing the worked example above: that same household with 15 GPG hardness and 1 ppm of iron has an effective hardness of 20 GPG once the iron adjustment is added. Daily load becomes 300 gallons times 20 GPG, or 6,000 grains a day, and 42,000 grains minimum capacity once multiplied by the 7-day regeneration cycle, a meaningfully bigger unit than the 31,500-grain figure hardness alone would suggest.

## What's the real difference between salt-based and salt-free systems?

Salt-based softeners run an ion-exchange process that swaps calcium and magnesium ions for sodium or potassium, physically removing hardness minerals from the water. Salt-free "conditioners" use template-assisted crystallization to alter mineral structure so it's less likely to stick to pipes, but the minerals stay in the water; a hardness test on treated water from a salt-free system reads the same as untreated water.

| Factor | Salt-based softener | Salt-free conditioner |
|---|---|---|
| Removes hardness minerals | Yes, via ion exchange | No, minerals stay in water |
| Effective hardness range | Any level | Reliable to about 10 GPG, degraded above 15 to 20 GPG |
| Regeneration | Needs salt and a drain connection | No regeneration cycle |
| NSF standard | NSF/ANSI 44 exists and applies | No equivalent NSF performance standard exists |
| Best fit | Hardness above 15 GPG, or any softening need | Mild scale prevention under about 10 GPG |

## Does NSF certification apply to salt-free conditioners?

Not the way it applies to salt-based units. NSF/ANSI 44 is the specific standard for water softeners using ion-exchange resin regenerated with sodium or potassium chloride, and certification against it means a product's hardness-reduction claims have been independently tested ([NSF, Standards for Water Treatment Systems](https://www.nsf.org/consumer-resources/articles/standards-water-treatment-systems)). No equivalent NSF performance standard currently exists for salt-free conditioning devices, so a "certified" claim on a salt-free product typically refers to component safety standards like NSF/ANSI 61, not a verified scale-prevention performance claim.

If your well water tests above 15 to 20 grains per gallon, most water treatment professionals consider a salt-free conditioner the wrong tool regardless of brand, since the technology's own limitations show up at that range.

## What does a well water softener system cost installed?

A complete well water softener system typically runs $2,000 to $5,000 installed, with most homeowners spending $2,500 to $3,500 for a quality salt-based system. Equipment alone runs $500 to $7,000 depending on grain capacity and single- versus dual-tank configuration, with installation labor adding $150 to $1,000. Ongoing costs include $5 to $15 a month in salt for salt-based systems, or $50 to $100 in periodic media replacement for salt-free systems, plus $300 to $600 a year in general maintenance.

## Single-tank vs dual-tank: which do you need?

A single-tank system, running $500 to $1,700, goes without soft water briefly during its regeneration cycle, typically overnight. A dual-tank system, running up to $5,000, keeps one tank in service while the other regenerates, so soft water is always available. Well water with heavier iron or higher daily household demand is where dual-tank setups earn their added cost; a light-use household on moderately hard water rarely needs one.

## FAQ

**How do I know what size water softener I need for well water?**
Multiply household size by 75 gallons per person, then by your tested hardness in grains per gallon, then by 7 for a weekly regeneration cycle. Add 5 grains per gallon to your hardness figure for every 1 ppm of iron present before doing the math.

**Is salt-free water conditioning as good as a salt-based softener for well water?**
Not for water above roughly 10 to 15 grains per gallon. Salt-free conditioners don't remove hardness minerals and lose reliability at higher hardness levels; a hardness test still reads hard water even after treatment.

**Does a water softener remove iron from well water?**
Only at low levels, generally up to about 3 ppm of clear-water ferrous iron, and only if you've sized the resin bed to account for that iron load. Higher iron levels need a dedicated iron filter ahead of the softener.

**How much does a well water softener cost per month to run?**
Roughly $5 to $15 a month in salt for a salt-based system, plus periodic resin or media costs, adding up to an estimated $300 to $600 a year in total maintenance for most households.

**Do I need NSF certification when buying a softener for well water?**
Yes, for salt-based units, look for NSF/ANSI 44 certification specifically, which verifies the hardness-reduction claim was independently tested. Salt-free conditioners don't have an equivalent performance certification to check.

Bottom line: size to hardness, daily use, and iron together, not household headcount alone, and pick salt-based over salt-free once your tested hardness runs above about 15 grains per gallon. Getting the grain capacity right the first time avoids a resin bed that's fighting above its rated load every single day.
