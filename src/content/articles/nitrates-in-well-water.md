# Nitrates in Well Water: Health Risks and How to Remove Them

By the WellWater HQ team

The EPA's enforceable limit for nitrate in drinking water is 10 milligrams per liter, measured as nitrogen, a threshold set specifically to protect infants from methemoglobinemia, also called blue baby syndrome ([EPA, Chemical Contaminant Rules](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/chemical-contaminant-rules), 2026). Private wells aren't legally bound by that limit the way public water systems are, but it's the number every lab, health department, and pediatrician uses to judge a private well's test result.

Nitrate is invisible, tasteless, and odorless, so a well can carry an elevated level for years without anyone noticing until a test flags it. Most contamination traces back to fertilizer, septic systems, or animal manure moving through soil into groundwater, which is why nitrate testing matters more in agricultural areas but isn't limited to them.

## What's the actual health risk from nitrate in well water?

Once ingested, nitrate converts to nitrite in the body, which interferes with how blood carries oxygen. In infants, this can cause methemoglobinemia: a condition where the blood loses its oxygen-carrying capacity, producing symptoms including shortness of breath and a bluish tint to the skin, sometimes over a period of just days ([EPA, Chemical Contaminant Rules](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/chemical-contaminant-rules), 2026).

Bottle-fed infants under six months old carry the highest risk, since their digestive systems haven't yet developed the ability to process nitrite the way older children and adults can. The [American Academy of Pediatrics recommends against using well water above 10 mg/L nitrate](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxprofiles/tp204-c8.pdf) for mixing infant formula or preparing baby food, regardless of whether an adult in the same household could drink the same water without symptoms (ATSDR, Nitrate and Nitrite Toxicity Profile, 2026). The EPA sets a separate, stricter enforceable limit for nitrite specifically, at 1 mg/L, reflecting how much more directly it affects blood oxygen transport than nitrate itself.

## Where does nitrate in well water come from?

Fertilizer applied to lawns, golf courses, and cropland is the most common source, since nitrogen-based fertilizers break down into nitrate that moves easily through soil with rainfall or irrigation. Septic systems and animal manure are the other two major sources, both of which release nitrogen compounds that convert to nitrate as they migrate toward groundwater.

Shallow wells, wells in sandy or fractured rock aquifers, and wells near agricultural land, feedlots, or septic drainfields carry the highest risk of nitrate contamination. A well casing that's cracked, poorly sealed, or improperly grouted also gives nitrate a more direct path down to the water table than a well constructed to current standards.

## Does boiling water remove nitrate?

No, and this is the most common misunderstanding about nitrate contamination. Boiling evaporates water without removing dissolved nitrate, which means the remaining water actually ends up with a higher nitrate concentration than before you started, not a lower one. State health departments are explicit on this point: [do not try to boil nitrate out of water](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/environment/water/wells/waterquality/nitrate.html), since boiling makes the problem worse, not better (Minnesota Department of Health, Nitrate in Well Water, 2025).

If your test comes back above 10 mg/L, the right immediate step is switching to bottled water for drinking, cooking, and infant formula, not reaching for the stove.

## What actually removes nitrate from well water?

| Removal method | How it works | Effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| Reverse osmosis | Forces water through a semi-permeable membrane, sized under NSF/ANSI 58 | High; typically removes most nitrate at the point of use |
| Anion exchange | Ion-exchange resin swaps nitrate ions for chloride | High, but requires resin sized to your specific nitrate level and regular regeneration |
| Distillation | Boils water and collects only the condensed vapor, leaving nitrate behind | High; nitrate does not evaporate with the water vapor, unlike simple boiling |

Reverse osmosis is usually installed at a single kitchen tap rather than whole-house, since nitrate removal only matters for water you actually drink or cook with, and RO membranes process water slowly. Anion exchange resin is nitrate-specific and different from the resin used in a standard water softener, so a softener alone will not remove nitrate even though both use ion exchange as the underlying mechanism.

## How is nitrate tested, and how often?

A certified laboratory test, not a home color-strip kit, gives the precise number you need to compare against the 10 mg/L limit, since strip-based nitrate tests can be hard to read accurately near the threshold that actually matters. The [EPA recommends testing private wells for nitrate annually](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water), alongside total coliform bacteria, total dissolved solids, and pH (EPA, Protect Your Home's Water, 2026), and testing immediately any time an infant will be drinking the water or a household member becomes pregnant.

## FAQ

**What nitrate level in well water is considered dangerous?**
The EPA's enforceable limit is 10 mg/L, measured as nitrogen, set specifically to protect infants from methemoglobinemia. Levels above that call for an alternate water source for infant formula and food preparation immediately, and treatment or a new source longer term.

**Can adults get sick from nitrate in well water?**
Adults tolerate nitrate at much higher levels than infants before showing symptoms, since the digestive process that converts nitrate to nitrite is less active in older children and adults. The EPA's 10 mg/L limit is specifically calibrated to protect the most vulnerable group, bottle-fed infants under six months.

**Does a water softener remove nitrate?**
No. A standard softener's resin is designed to swap calcium and magnesium ions for sodium, not nitrate for chloride. Nitrate removal requires either reverse osmosis, a nitrate-specific anion exchange resin, or distillation.

**How quickly can nitrate levels change in a well?**
They can shift meaningfully within a single year, especially after heavy fertilizer application, flooding, or changes in nearby land use, which is part of why annual retesting matters even for a well that's tested clean in the past.

**Is it safe to drink well water with nitrate if I'm not pregnant or feeding an infant?**
At levels near or above 10 mg/L, the EPA's guidance doesn't distinguish by household member; the enforceable limit applies to the water itself. Anyone in a household with elevated nitrate should treat the water or switch sources, since risk factors like pregnancy can also change over time.

Bottom line: 10 mg/L is the number that matters, boiling makes a nitrate problem worse instead of better, and reverse osmosis or nitrate-specific anion exchange are the only methods that actually remove it. Test annually, and treat any result near the limit as reason enough to switch water sources for infants right away.
