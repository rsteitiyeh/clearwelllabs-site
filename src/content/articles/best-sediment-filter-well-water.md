By the WellWater HQ team

The best sediment filter for well water is sized to your flow rate and sediment load, not picked by brand. Most wells do well with a 5-micron cartridge in a 4.5 by 20 inch housing; heavy sediment needs a coarser pre-filter stage first, and fine 1-micron filtration works best right before a UV system.

Micron rating and gallons-per-minute capacity determine performance, not price. A filter rated too fine for a sandy well clogs in hours and starves your fixtures of pressure. One rated too coarse lets grit through to your water heater, washing machine, and faucet aerators.

## What micron rating should a well water sediment filter use?

5 microns is the standard choice for most private wells, catching fine silt and rust particles without excessive pressure drop or constant cartridge changes. If you need water clear enough for a UV disinfection unit downstream, step down to 1 micron, since UV systems need low turbidity to work reliably.

For wells with heavy sediment, a single fine filter clogs within days. The better setup runs a coarse 50-micron spin-down filter ahead of the pressure tank, followed by a 5-micron cartridge housing after it. The spin-down catches the bulk of the grit and can be flushed by hand, so the finer cartridge downstream lasts months instead of days.

## How do you size the housing to your well's flow rate?

Match the housing to your tested peak flow rate, not the size that happened to be in stock. Most residential wells run fine on a 4.5 by 20 inch Big Blue housing, which handles roughly 10 to 15 GPM without a significant pressure drop.

If your well's peak draw runs higher than that, either step up to a larger housing or add a second housing in parallel rather than forcing all the flow through undersized cartridge media. To measure your flow rate, run a garden hose at full blast into a 5-gallon bucket for 60 seconds with nothing else running; the gallons collected is your working GPM number.

## What's the difference between a spin-down filter and a cartridge filter?

A spin-down filter is a reusable, coarse first stage, typically 50 to 100 microns, that you flush by hand and never throw away. A cartridge filter is a finer, disposable second stage that actually gets replaced on a schedule.

Using both in sequence is the standard setup for a well with visible sand or grit; using only a fine cartridge without a pre-filter is the most common reason well owners end up changing cartridges every few weeks instead of every few months.

## How often do sediment filter cartridges need replacing?

Replace a sediment cartridge when household water pressure drops 5 to 10 PSI below normal, or every 3 to 6 months, whichever happens first. Sandier wells and higher household usage push that toward the shorter end of the range.

## Does a sediment filter remove iron, bacteria, or hardness?

No. A sediment filter is a mechanical, particle-only stage; it strains out sand, silt, and rust particles but does nothing to dissolved iron, bacteria, or hardness minerals. Those need separate treatment: oxidation or ion exchange for iron, disinfection for bacteria, and a softener or conditioner for hardness.

The EPA's own guidance on private wells lists "stained plumbing fixtures" as a reason to test for iron, copper, and manganese specifically, separate from sediment concerns ([EPA, Protect Your Home's Water](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water)). If your water looks cloudy and also stains fixtures or smells off, you likely need a sediment filter plus a second treatment stage, not a bigger sediment filter alone.

## FAQ

**What is the best micron rating for well water?**
5 microns for most wells, the balance point between catching fine sediment and keeping flow rate high. Use 20 to 50 microns as a pre-filter stage if your well runs heavy sediment, and 1 micron only right before equipment like UV that needs very clear water.

**Do I need a sediment filter if my well water looks clear?**
Often yes. Fine silt and grit can be present without visible cloudiness and still cause premature wear on faucet cartridges, water heater elements, and washing machine valves over time.

**Can one sediment filter handle a whole house?**
Yes, if the housing and cartridge are sized to your tested peak flow rate. A single properly sized 4.5 by 20 inch housing handles most homes; higher-flow homes need a larger housing or a parallel setup.

**How do I know when to change the cartridge?**
Watch household water pressure. A drop of 5 to 10 PSI from normal, or reaching the 3 to 6 month mark, both signal it's time regardless of how the cartridge looks.

**Will a sediment filter remove well water odor?**
No. Odor usually comes from dissolved gas like hydrogen sulfide or from bacteria, neither of which a mechanical sediment filter addresses. That needs aeration, oxidation, or a carbon stage depending on the cause.

Bottom line: pick the micron rating and housing size for your tested flow and sediment load first, then compare brands within that spec. A correctly sized $250 setup beats an oversold $600 unit that's still choking on your well's real sediment load.
