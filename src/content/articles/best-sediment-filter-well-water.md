The best sediment filter for well water depends on what your well sends up. Heavy sand calls for a flushable spin-down screen at 100 to 500 microns. Fine silt needs a pleated or depth cartridge at 30 microns down to 1. Most sandy wells do best with staged filtration, coarse to fine, sized to household flow.

What do micron ratings actually mean?

A micron is one millionth of a meter. A human hair runs about 70 microns across. Beach sand sits around 100 to 2,000 microns, fine silt around 2 to 50, and clay particles below 2. A filter rated at 50 microns catches particles bigger than that and lets smaller ones through.

There is a catch worth knowing. Most cheap cartridges carry nominal ratings, meaning they catch a majority of particles at the stated size, not all of them. Absolute-rated filters catch 99 percent or better but cost more and clog faster. For sediment duty on a well, nominal is usually fine. Save absolute ratings for protecting UV systems or reverse osmosis membranes downstream.

The mistake we see most often is going too fine too fast. A 5 micron cartridge as the first line of defense on a sandy well will clog in days, drop your water pressure, and have you buying cartridges by the case. Coarse first, fine second. Always.

Spin-down filters: the workhorse for sand

A spin-down filter spins incoming water inside a clear housing so heavier particles drop into a collection chamber, with a stainless screen catching the rest. Ratings run 100 to 500 microns. The killer feature is the flush valve at the bottom: open it for ten seconds and the trapped sand blasts out to a drain. No cartridge to buy, ever.

Some models add an automatic flush timer, which is worth the upgrade if your well moves serious sand. The screen itself lasts years. If your pressure gauge shows a growing drop across the filter even after flushing, the screen is scaled or damaged and a $10 to $20 replacement fixes it.

Pleated cartridges: the mid-range catcher

Pleated cartridges fold a polyester sheet into an accordion, giving lots of surface area in a standard housing. Typical sediment duty ratings are 30 to 50 microns. Surface area means capacity: a pleated cartridge holds several times the dirt of a string-wound filter at the same rating before pressure drops.

They are also rinseable. Pull the cartridge, hose off the pleats, and reuse it two or three times before the media breaks down. On a moderately silty well that stretches a $15 cartridge across most of a year.

String-wound and melt-blown depth filters: the fine polish

Depth filters trap particles through the full thickness of the media rather than on the surface. String-wound cartridges wrap cotton or polypropylene cord around a core; melt-blown cartridges are extruded foam-like tubes. Both commonly come in 1 and 5 micron ratings, which is where you go to catch fine silt and to protect UV sterilizers, which need clear water to disinfect properly.

They cannot be washed. Once loaded, they go in the trash, so put them last in line where the coarse stuff has already been removed and they only see fine particles.

Staged filtration: coarse to fine

On a well with real sediment problems, the winning setup is a train: spin-down at 250 microns, pleated at 30, then depth at 5 or 1 if downstream equipment needs it. Each stage catches what it is sized for, so the fine cartridges last months instead of days. Total hardware runs $150 to $500 depending on housing sizes, which is cheap insurance for everything behind it.

How do you size a sediment filter to household flow?

Undersized filters cause more misery than wrong micron ratings. Every filter creates some pressure drop, and the drop grows as flow rises and the filter loads. A standard 10 inch by 2.5 inch cartridge housing handles roughly 5 to 10 gallons per minute comfortably. A 4.5 by 20 inch "big blue" housing handles 15 to 25 gpm and holds far more dirt between changes.

Estimate your peak demand by counting fixtures that run at once. A shower is about 2 gpm, a washing machine 3 to 5, a couple of sinks 1 to 2 each. A family of four routinely hits 8 to 12 gpm on a busy morning. That puts most households at the edge of a 10 inch housing, which is why we usually point people with more than two bathrooms toward big blue housings. The larger cartridge also changes far less often, and cartridge changes are the chore people actually skip.

Match the housing ports to your plumbing too. A 1 inch ported housing on a 1 inch main keeps velocity down. Necking a 1 inch line through a 3/4 inch filter port throttles the whole house.

When does a sediment filter protect downstream equipment?

Sediment is rarely a health issue by itself, but it is the number one killer of the equipment behind it. A sediment stage ahead of these components pays for itself:

  - Water softeners: grit scores the control valve pistons and fouls resin beds. A 30 to 50 micron prefilter extends softener life by years.

  - Iron filters and backwashing media tanks: sand accumulates in the media and channels the flow, cutting treatment performance.

  - UV sterilizers: particles shadow microbes from the UV light. Makers require 5 micron prefiltration for the disinfection rating to hold.

  - Pressure tanks, valves, and fixtures: sand chews through check valves, fill valves, and cartridge faucets.

  - Tankless water heaters: fine scale and grit clog the heat exchanger inlet screens.

First, find out what you are filtering

A sediment filter treats a symptom. New or worsening sand often means a degrading well screen, a pump set too low, or a collapsing casing, and those get more expensive the longer they run. Test the water too, not just for grit. CDC and EPA recommend testing private wells at least annually for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH (https://www.epa.gov/privatewells and https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/private-wells.html). Cloudy water after storms can signal surface water reaching the well, which is a bacteria pathway. For anything health-related, use a state-certified lab or contact your local health department.

Common questions

What micron rating should a sediment filter be for well water?

Start coarse. If you see visible sand, begin with a 100 to 250 micron spin-down. For cloudy silt, add a 30 to 50 micron pleated cartridge behind it. Only go to 5 or 1 micron when protecting UV or reverse osmosis equipment. A single fine filter as the first stage clogs fast and drops pressure.

Is a spin-down filter better than a cartridge filter?

For sand, yes. The flushable screen means no recurring cartridge cost and ten-second maintenance. For fine silt below roughly 100 microns, a spin-down cannot catch it and a pleated or depth cartridge takes over. Many wells need both, plumbed coarse to fine.

How often should sediment filter cartridges be changed?

Watch pressure, not the calendar. A gauge on each side of the housing tells you when the drop exceeds about 10 to 15 psi, which is change time. As a rough schedule, depth cartridges last 1 to 3 months on sandy wells and pleated cartridges 3 to 6, longer with a spin-down ahead of them.

Do I need a big blue housing or a standard 10 inch?

Count your peak flow. Households that run under about 8 gpm at peak do fine with a standard 10 by 2.5 inch housing. Bigger families, homes with more than two bathrooms, or anyone tired of monthly cartridge changes should step up to a 4.5 by 20 inch big blue, which flows 15 to 25 gpm with less pressure drop.

Will a sediment filter fix cloudy well water?

If the cloudiness is suspended silt, a staged setup ending at 5 microns usually clears it. If water turns cloudy after rain, test for bacteria first, since that pattern can mean surface water is entering the well. Persistent milky water that clears from the bottom up is just dissolved air, and no filter is needed.

Bottom line

The best sediment filter for well water is usually two filters: a flushable spin-down at 100 to 250 microns doing the heavy lifting, and a 30 to 50 micron pleated cartridge polishing behind it, stepping down to 5 microns only when UV or RO equipment demands it. Size the housing to peak flow, put a pressure gauge on each side, and change cartridges when the drop hits 10 to 15 psi.
