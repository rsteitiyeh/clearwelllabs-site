# Low Water Pressure From a Well: Causes and Fixes

By the WellWater HQ team

Low water pressure from a well traces to one of roughly six causes: a pressure switch set too low, a waterlogged pressure tank, a clogged sediment filter, mineral scale narrowing old pipes, a pump losing capacity as it wears, or the well's yield declining seasonally. The fix depends entirely on which one it is, so the first step isn't grabbing a wrench, it's narrowing down where the problem actually sits.

Start by figuring out whether the drop is at one fixture or the whole house. A single low-pressure faucet or showerhead is almost always local, a clogged aerator or a failing cartridge, not a well system problem at all. Weak pressure at every fixture in the house is the whole-house case this guide covers.

## Is the low pressure at one fixture or the whole house?

Check a second fixture, ideally on a different floor or a different plumbing run, before assuming it's a well problem. If only one faucet or showerhead is weak, remove and clean the aerator or showerhead screen; sediment and mineral buildup collect there first and are the single most common cause of a one-fixture pressure complaint.

If every fixture in the house runs weak, especially if it happened gradually rather than overnight, move to the whole-house causes below. A sudden, house-wide drop points toward the pressure switch or tank; a gradual decline over months or years points more toward pipe scale, a wearing pump, or the well's seasonal yield.

## What causes sudden, house-wide low pressure?

A pressure switch cut-out set lower than it should be, or a switch that's failing, is the most common sudden cause; check the gauge on your pressure tank against the switch's rated cut-in and cut-out settings, usually 20/40, 30/50, or 40/60 psi. A waterlogged pressure tank, one that's lost its internal air charge, is the second most common: it makes the pump short-cycle and never lets pressure build to the normal cut-out point before the pump kicks back on.

A clogged sediment or whole-house filter cartridge is the third common sudden cause, since a filter that's overdue for a change restricts flow across the entire house at once. Check the filter's pressure gauges, if it has them, or simply pull the cartridge and look at it; a cartridge packed with sediment is an easy visual confirmation.

## What causes gradual, long-term low pressure?

Mineral scale building up inside old galvanized steel pipe narrows the pipe's usable inside diameter year over year, which reduces flow even though nothing about the well or pump has changed. This is a plumbing problem, not a well problem, and no pump or tank adjustment fixes it; the pipe section itself needs replacing, usually with copper or PEX.

A pump losing capacity as its impeller wears is a separate gradual cause, distinct from a pump that's failed outright and produces no water at all. A worn pump still runs and still delivers water, just at a lower flow rate and pressure than it used to, which is easy to mistake for a tank or switch problem until you rule those out first.

## Can a well's water yield cause low pressure?

Yes, particularly in shallow or lower-yield wells during dry summer months, when the water table can drop closer to or below the pump's intake depth. Penn State Extension's guidance on low-yielding wells notes that a well drawn down faster than the aquifer can recharge it will show reduced pressure and flow, sometimes seasonally and predictably, even with a fully functional pump and tank ([Penn State Extension, Using Low-Yielding Wells](https://extension.psu.edu/using-low-yielding-wells), 2026).

If your low pressure shows up reliably in the same season each year and resolves on its own afterward, that pattern points to well yield rather than equipment, and the long-term fix is either a storage/cistern system or, in persistent cases, a deeper well.

## Diagnostic table: matching the symptom to the cause

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| One fixture only | Clogged aerator or cartridge | Clean or replace the fixture part |
| Sudden, whole house | Pressure switch or waterlogged tank | Check switch settings; test tank's Schrader valve |
| Sudden, whole house, after a filter change is overdue | Clogged sediment/whole-house filter | Replace the cartridge |
| Gradual, over months to years | Pipe scale in old galvanized plumbing | Replace the scaled pipe section |
| Gradual, low flow but pump still runs | Worn pump impeller | Have a well contractor test flow rate and pump output |
| Seasonal, same time each year | Declining well yield | Storage/cistern system, or a deeper well long term |

## When should you call a well contractor instead of DIY-ing it?

Call a professional once you've ruled out the aerator, filter cartridge, and an obviously waterlogged tank, and pressure is still low. Pump capacity testing, pressure switch replacement involving live electrical contacts, and any diagnosis of well yield all require equipment and training a homeowner doesn't have on hand, and a wrong guess on any of them wastes money on parts that weren't the problem.

Run your gauge readings and switch settings through the WellWater HQ well pressure diagnostic checklist before you call, so the contractor starts from what you've already ruled out instead of from scratch. ## FAQ

**Why is my well water pressure suddenly low?**
A sudden, whole-house drop is most often a pressure switch set too low, a waterlogged pressure tank that's lost its air charge, or a sediment filter overdue for a change. Check the tank's pressure gauge and the filter cartridge first.

**Why has my well water pressure gotten weaker over time?**
Gradual pressure loss over months or years usually means scale building up inside old galvanized pipe, narrowing the usable diameter, or a pump losing capacity as its impeller wears. Neither fixes itself, and neither is a tank or switch problem.

**Can low water pressure mean my well is running dry?**
It can, especially in shallow wells during dry summer months when the water table drops closer to the pump intake. If the low pressure follows a predictable seasonal pattern, well yield is a likely cause.

**Is low pressure at just one faucet a well problem?**
No, almost always not. A single weak fixture points to a clogged aerator or a failing cartridge inside that specific faucet or showerhead, not the well system. Check a second fixture before assuming it's whole-house.

**How do I know if it's the pressure switch or the pressure tank causing low pressure?**
Compare the gauge reading against the switch's rated cut-in and cut-out settings; a mismatch points to the switch. Check the tank's Schrader valve for a waterlogged bladder separately, since either one alone, or both together, can cause the same symptom.

Bottom line: localize the problem to one fixture or the whole house first, then work through the sudden-cause list before the gradual-cause list. Most low-pressure calls trace to a switch, a tank, or a filter, all things worth checking before assuming the well itself is the problem.
