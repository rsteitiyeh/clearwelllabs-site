# Well Water Smells Like Rotten Eggs: Causes and Fixes

By the WellWater HQ team

A rotten egg smell in well water is almost always hydrogen sulfide gas (H2S), produced either by naturally occurring sulfur bacteria in the groundwater or well, or by a chemical reaction inside your water heater between its magnesium anode rod and sulfate in the water. Which one it is changes the fix: a water heater problem needs an anode swap or a heater flush, while a well or plumbing problem needs shock chlorination or a dedicated treatment system.

Sulfur bacteria themselves aren't a health concern, and in most cases the smell has nothing to do with whether the water is safe to drink. Still, hydrogen sulfide gas can be genuinely hazardous in enclosed spaces like well pits and basements, so it's worth taking seriously as a gas even when it's harmless as a taste-and-odor problem in your glass.

## How do you tell whether the smell is coming from the water heater, the softener, or the well?

Smell water from each faucet separately after the house has sat unused for a few hours, starting with an outside faucet ahead of any treatment equipment.

- **Smell only from hot water taps, not cold:** the problem is almost always the water heater's magnesium anode rod reacting with sulfate.
- **Smell from both hot and cold, but not from faucets that bypass the water softener:** sulfur bacteria have colonized the softener resin bed.
- **Smell from every faucet, softener or not, and it fades after the tap runs a minute:** the source is hydrogen sulfide gas already dissolved in the groundwater.
- **Smell from every faucet and it doesn't fade:** sulfur bacteria have colonized the well casing or household plumbing itself, not just the groundwater passing through.

This diagnostic sequence follows guidance from the [Minnesota Department of Health on hydrogen sulfide in well water](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/environment/water/wells/waterquality/hydrosulfide.html) (Minnesota Department of Health, Hydrogen Sulfide and Sulfur Bacteria in Well Water, 2024).

## What's actually producing the smell?

Sulfate-reducing bacteria are the most common cause. They thrive in the low-oxygen environment inside a well casing or plumbing system, converting sulfate naturally present in groundwater into hydrogen sulfide gas as part of their normal metabolism. This process is most common in wells drilled into shale or sandstone bedrock.

A water heater's anode rod is the second most common cause and the easiest to isolate, since it only affects hot water. In rare cases, the smell traces back to sewage or other pollution rather than natural sulfur bacteria, which is why a rotten-egg smell is also a reasonable trigger to test your water for coliform bacteria and nitrate, even though the smell itself usually isn't dangerous.

## Is hydrogen sulfide in well water dangerous?

Not usually, at the concentrations typically found in household water. There's no EPA Maximum Contaminant Level for hydrogen sulfide because it's a taste-and-odor issue rather than a regulated health contaminant at those levels. The bigger risk is hydrogen sulfide gas building up in confined, poorly ventilated spaces like well pits, where it can reach hazardous concentrations; well pits should only ever be entered by a professional using proper safety equipment.

Because a rotten-egg smell can, rarely, coincide with sewage contamination, testing for total coliform bacteria alongside addressing the odor is worth the extra step rather than assuming the smell alone rules out a real problem.

## What treats hydrogen sulfide, and at what concentration?

| Concentration range | Effective treatment |
|---|---|
| Under 1 mg/L | Activated carbon filter |
| Under 6 mg/L | Oxidizing filter (manganese greensand media) |
| Up to 10 mg/L | Ozone treatment with filtration |
| Any level, transient sources | Shock chlorination (temporary; best for biofouling, not a permanent fix on its own) |

Table adapted from [well-industry guidance published by Wellowner.org](https://wellowner.org/resources/water-quality/contaminants/hydrogen-sulfide/), a National Ground Water Association resource for private well owners (Wellowner.org, Hydrogen Sulfide in Water, 2026). A water treatment professional can test your actual concentration and match the technology to it rather than guessing from the table alone.

## How do you fix a smell that's only in the hot water?

Replace the water heater's magnesium anode rod with one made of aluminum or zinc, which corrodes without producing hydrogen sulfide gas at the same rate. Alternatively, a plumber can disinfect and flush the water heater with a chlorine bleach solution, which kills the sulfur bacteria but may let the smell return within weeks if the anode reaction is the real driver.

Raising the water heater's temperature to 160F for several hours also kills the bacteria, but this step needs a manufacturer-approved pressure relief valve and careful monitoring, since it creates a scalding hazard; have a plumber handle this rather than doing it yourself.

## How do you fix it if it's coming from the well or plumbing?

Shock chlorination, the same process used for a positive coliform test, kills the sulfur bacteria present at the time of treatment and is often the first step, but sulfur bacteria can be stubborn to fully clear once established, sometimes needing repeat treatments or pre-work like scrubbing the casing. For hydrogen sulfide dissolved in the groundwater itself rather than bacterial colonization, an ongoing treatment system (carbon, oxidizing filtration, or aeration) sized to your actual concentration is the long-term fix, not a one-time disinfection.

## FAQ

**Why does my well water only smell bad from the hot water tap?**
That pattern points to your water heater's magnesium anode rod reacting with sulfate in the water to produce hydrogen sulfide gas. Replacing the anode with an aluminum or zinc rod, or flushing the heater with a chlorine solution, typically resolves it.

**Is hydrogen sulfide in well water bad for my health?**
Not usually, at typical household concentrations; it's mainly a taste-and-odor nuisance rather than a regulated health contaminant. The gas can be hazardous in enclosed spaces like well pits, so leave that area to a professional if you suspect a buildup there.

**Will a water softener fix the rotten egg smell?**
Only if the smell is caused by sulfur bacteria that colonized the softener's resin bed itself, in which case disinfecting the softener per the manufacturer's instructions helps. A softener won't treat hydrogen sulfide dissolved in the groundwater; that needs carbon, oxidizing, or aeration treatment instead.

**Does shock chlorination permanently remove the rotten egg smell?**
Sometimes, especially for a bacterial source in the well or plumbing, but sulfur bacteria can be persistent and may need repeat treatment. If hydrogen sulfide is dissolved in the groundwater itself, chlorination alone won't be a lasting fix; you'll need ongoing filtration sized to your concentration.

**Should I test my water if it smells like rotten eggs?**
Yes. The smell itself is usually harmless, but because it can rarely coincide with sewage contamination, testing for total coliform bacteria and nitrate alongside addressing the odor is worth doing rather than assuming the smell explains everything.

Bottom line: figure out whether the smell follows the hot water, the softener, or every tap before you buy anything, since that single diagnostic step points straight to whether the fix is an anode rod, a disinfection, or an ongoing filtration system. Guessing at the fix without isolating the source wastes money on the wrong equipment.
