# Acidic Well Water: Causes, Testing, and Correction

By the WellWater HQ team

Acidic well water tests below 7.0 on the pH scale, and the EPA's secondary standard for drinking water recommends a range of 6.5 to 8.5 ([EPA, Secondary Drinking Water Standards](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals), 2026). Below 6.5, the water itself isn't usually the health risk. What it does to copper pipe, brass fittings, and any lead solder in older plumbing is.

pH isn't a contaminant the EPA regulates as a primary standard, so there's no enforceable limit and no fine for having low pH water. That's exactly why it goes unnoticed for years in a lot of homes: nothing about acidic water fails a required test, until someone notices blue-green stains in the sink or a plumber finds a pinhole leak in a copper line that's only a few years old.

## What causes acidic well water?

Groundwater picks up its pH from the rock and soil it moves through. Water sitting in or passing through granite, sandstone, or areas with thin, acidic topsoil, common across New England, the Appalachians, and parts of the Pacific Northwest, tends to come out of the ground already below neutral. Rain itself is mildly acidic before it even reaches the aquifer, and in areas with little limestone or calcium carbonate to buffer it along the way, that acidity carries straight through to the well.

Shallow wells and wells in granite bedrock are the most consistent offenders. A well in limestone country, by contrast, usually tests neutral to slightly alkaline, since the rock itself neutralizes acidity as water passes through it.

## How can you tell if your well water is acidic?

The most reliable early signs show up on fixtures, not in the water's taste. Blue-green staining around drains, faucet aerators, and in the bathtub points to copper corrosion. A metallic or sour taste, water that turns clothes a faint blue-green in the wash, or pinhole leaks developing in copper supply lines within just a handful of years are all downstream symptoms of low pH doing its work on the plumbing.

None of those signs tell you the actual number, though, and correction only works if it's dosed to the right target.

## What does acidic well water actually do to plumbing and health?

Water below pH 6.5 becomes corrosive to copper pipe, brass fittings, and galvanized steel, and the corrosion accelerates as pH drops further. That matters beyond the cost of replacing pipe: in homes with lead solder joints (common in plumbing installed before 1986) or leaded brass fixtures, acidic water can leach lead directly into the water people drink. The CDC's guidance on groundwater corrosivity is direct on this connection, noting that corrosive water is the mechanism that pulls lead out of old solder and fittings and into tap water, not just the presence of lead in the plumbing itself ([CDC, Groundwater Corrosivity and Lead in Wells](https://www.cdc.gov/environmental-health-services/php/water/groundwater-corrosivity-lead-wells.html), 2026). The EPA sets a copper action level of 1.3 mg/L for exactly this reason, an action level built around corrosion control rather than copper occurring naturally in groundwater ([EPA, Basic Information About Lead in Drinking Water](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/basic-information-about-lead-drinking-water), 2026).

## How do you test well water pH?

A basic pH test strip from a hardware store gives a rough read, but for anything close to a treatment decision, a certified lab test is worth the cost, since strips are hard to read precisely near the ranges that matter (6.0 to 6.9 looks nearly identical on most consumer strips). Test at the tap closest to where water enters the house, and test again after any major rain event or seasonal thaw, since shallow wells in particular can shift half a point or more between wet and dry seasons.

| pH range | What it means | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Below 5.0 | Severely acidic; rapid corrosion of copper and brass | Soda ash injection, sized to the specific reading |
| 5.0 to 6.4 | Moderately acidic; steady corrosion, staining likely | Calcite neutralizer, or soda ash if hardness is already high |
| 6.5 to 8.5 | EPA's recommended range; not corrosive | No correction needed |
| Above 8.5 | Alkaline; scale buildup, soapy taste | Acid injection (rare in private wells) |

## How do you correct acidic well water?

Calcite neutralizers are the standard fix for water in the 5.0 to 6.9 range, and they work by dissolving calcium carbonate media as water passes through a tank, which raises pH and adds a small amount of hardness back into the water. Calcite manufacturer specs rate the media as reliable up to about pH 6.9 with calcium hardness under 150 mg/L; below pH 6.0, calcite alone often can't raise the water enough, and a soda ash injection feeder becomes the more consistent option (Clean Water Store, How to Treat Acid Well Water, 2026). Soda ash (sodium carbonate) is metered in with a small chemical feed pump and raises pH without adding hardness, which also makes it the better choice for a household that's already dealing with hard water on top of low pH.

Both systems need upkeep: calcite media gets topped off roughly once a year as it dissolves, and soda ash solution tanks need refilling every three to six months depending on household water use. Neither system removes lead already in the plumbing; correcting pH stops new corrosion, it doesn't reverse damage already done to old solder joints.

## FAQ

**Is acidic well water safe to drink?**
Low pH water itself isn't the primary concern; what it does to plumbing is. In homes with lead solder or older brass fittings, acidic water can leach lead into tap water over time, which is a genuine health concern, especially for children and pregnant women.

**What pH is considered acidic for well water?**
Anything below 7.0 is technically acidic, but the EPA's recommended range is 6.5 to 8.5. Water below 6.5 is where corrosion of copper and brass plumbing typically starts to accelerate.

**Can I raise my well water pH myself?**
A calcite neutralizer tank is a straightforward DIY-adjacent install for moderately acidic water (pH 5.0 to 6.9), though sizing it to your actual flow rate and test result matters. Water below pH 5.0, or households with existing hardness above 150 mg/L, usually do better with a professionally sized soda ash system.

**How often should I retest after installing a neutralizer?**
Test again about a month after installation to confirm the system is holding pH in the target range, then annually after that, since calcite media efficiency drops gradually as it dissolves.

**Does acidic well water affect a septic system?**
Not directly. pH correction is about protecting household plumbing and drinking water, not septic function, though extremely low pH can affect the bacterial balance a septic system relies on in rare cases.

Bottom line: below pH 6.5, well water starts corroding copper and brass, and in homes with old solder, that corrosion is what puts lead in the water, not the well itself. Test with a lab, not just a strip, and match the fix to the number: calcite above pH 5.0, soda ash below it.
