# Reverse Osmosis for Well Water: Is It Worth It

By the WellWater HQ team

Reverse osmosis is worth it for a well with arsenic, nitrate, or high total dissolved solids above what a standard filter or softener can touch, and not worth it as a whole-house solution for most wells, since it's slow, wastes water, and is normally installed at a single drinking-water tap instead. A certified under-sink unit runs $150 to $1,200 in equipment plus $150 to $300 in labor if you don't install it yourself.

Well water isn't the same feed as city water, and that's where a lot of RO buying guides fall short. Iron, manganese, and sediment common in well water will foul an RO membrane fast if you skip pretreatment, so the honest answer to "is it worth it" depends as much on what's ahead of the RO unit as the unit itself.

## What does reverse osmosis actually remove from well water?

RO forces water through a semi-permeable membrane fine enough to block dissolved solids, arsenic, nitrate, fluoride, lead, and most other dissolved contaminants, along with sediment and most microorganisms as a secondary effect of the membrane's pore size. Certified point-of-use RO systems are tested against NSF/ANSI 58, the standard that governs performance claims for contaminant reduction and structural integrity in residential RO units ([NSF, NSF/ANSI 58: Reverse Osmosis Drinking Water Treatment Systems](https://www.nsf.org/knowledge-library/nsf-ansi-58-reverse-osmosis-drinking-water-treatment-systems), 2026).

What it does not reliably remove: dissolved gases like radon or hydrogen sulfide, which pass through the membrane rather than getting trapped by it. RO also isn't a substitute for disinfection on a well with a positive coliform test; treat the biological problem first, then add RO for the dissolved contaminants a disinfection step won't touch.

## Why does well water need pretreatment before an RO membrane?

Because iron, manganese, and sediment, all common in well water and rare in treated city supply, physically clog and chemically foul an RO membrane within weeks if they hit it untreated. A sediment prefilter and, where iron or manganese show up on your test, a dedicated iron filter ahead of the RO unit protect the membrane and are the difference between a 2 to 3 year membrane life and one that fails in a few months.

This is the step most generic RO buying guides skip, since they're written for people on municipal water that's already been filtered and disinfected upstream. On a well, pretreatment isn't optional; it's what makes the membrane's rated lifespan realistic.

## Does reverse osmosis remove arsenic and nitrate from well water?

Yes, RO is one of the three EPA-recognized effective methods for both, alongside anion exchange and, for nitrate specifically, distillation. Arsenic removal, though, depends on its oxidation state: arsenite (arsenic III) carries no electrical charge and passes through an RO membrane far less completely than arsenate (arsenic V), which does. Reverse osmosis alone typically removes 60 to 80 percent of arsenic III, but pairing it with a pre-oxidation step to convert arsenic III to arsenic V pushes removal to 95 to 99 percent, according to water treatment engineering literature on arsenic oxidation and filtration methods ([ScienceDirect, Removal of arsenic as a potentially toxic element from drinking water by filtration](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844023014536), 2023).

A certified lab test that reports arsenic speciation, not just total arsenic, tells you whether your well needs that extra oxidation step or whether standard RO alone will hit the EPA's 0.010 mg/L limit.

## How much water does an RO system waste?

| System type | Wastewater ratio | Gallons wasted per gallon produced |
|---|---|---|
| Older/standard residential RO | 3:1 to 4:1 | 3 to 4 gallons |
| Newer high-efficiency RO | 1:1 to 1.5:1 | 1 to 1.5 gallons |

Older systems send 3 to 4 gallons down the drain for every gallon of treated water they produce; newer designs with a permeate pump or non-electric booster cut that closer to 1:1. On a well drawing from a limited-yield aquifer or a shared water source, that ratio is worth checking before you buy, since it affects both your well's daily draw and your septic system's intake if you're on one.

## What does an RO system cost to buy, install, and run?

An under-sink RO system runs $150 to $600 for a mid-range 4 to 6 stage unit, or up to $1,200 for a tankless/high-efficiency model, plus $150 to $300 in professional install labor for a straightforward under-sink hookup. Whole-house RO exists but is rare and expensive for residential wells, typically $3,000 to $15,000 installed, since it needs a much larger membrane bank and a booster pump to push whole-house flow rates through it.

Ongoing costs: prefilter cartridges every 6 to 12 months, roughly $10 to $40 each, and the RO membrane itself every 2 to 3 years, roughly $30 to $100. Budget for a sediment or iron prefilter change on the same schedule if your well water carries either.

## Is reverse osmosis worth it for your well?

Worth it if your test shows arsenic, nitrate, fluoride, or TDS above what you're comfortable drinking, and you're willing to run a sediment or iron prefilter ahead of it. Not worth it as your only treatment if your problem is bacteria, since RO isn't a certified disinfection method on its own, or if your only complaint is hardness or iron staining, both of which a softener or iron filter handles more efficiently and at lower ongoing cost.

## FAQ

**Does reverse osmosis remove all contaminants from well water?**
No. It removes most dissolved solids, arsenic, nitrate, fluoride, and lead, but dissolved gases like radon and hydrogen sulfide pass through the membrane, and it isn't a certified substitute for disinfecting a well with confirmed bacteria.

**Can you install reverse osmosis directly on well water without any other treatment?**
Not safely for the membrane's lifespan. Sediment and iron common in well water will foul an untreated RO membrane within weeks to months; a sediment prefilter, and an iron filter if your test shows iron or manganese, should sit ahead of it.

**Is whole-house reverse osmosis worth it for a well?**
Rarely. Whole-house RO costs $3,000 to $15,000 installed and wastes far more water than a single point-of-use unit at the kitchen tap, which is where nearly all drinking-water contaminant exposure actually happens.

**How long does an RO membrane last on well water?**
Typically 2 to 3 years with proper prefiltration; without it, iron and sediment fouling can cut that to a few months. Prefilter cartridges need replacement every 6 to 12 months regardless.

**Does reverse osmosis remove arsenic from well water completely?**
It removes most arsenate (arsenic V) but only 60 to 80 percent of arsenite (arsenic III) without pre-oxidation. A lab test that reports arsenic speciation tells you whether your well needs that extra step to reliably hit the EPA's limit.

Bottom line: RO earns its cost on a well with arsenic, nitrate, or high TDS, provided you protect the membrane with sediment or iron pretreatment first. For hardness or iron alone, a dedicated softener or iron filter does the job cheaper and with less wasted water.
