Well water inspection cost runs from about $75 for a basic flow test to $400 or so for a standard inspection, and $650 to $1,000 or more once you add certified lab testing or a combined well and septic package for a home loan. Local rates vary a lot, so get at least two quotes.

What does a well inspection actually cover?

A real well inspection is more than a guy glancing at your wellhead. When we walk a property with an inspector, the visit usually breaks into three parts: a flow and yield test, an equipment check, and water quality sampling.

The flow test measures how many gallons per minute the well can deliver over a sustained draw, often 30 to 60 minutes. Most lenders and county health departments want to see 3 to 5 gpm sustained for a single family home, though standards differ by state. A well that produces 10 gpm for two minutes and then sputters is a very different asset than one that holds 5 gpm all afternoon.

The equipment check covers the pump, pressure tank, pressure switch, wiring, the well cap and casing, and the pitless adapter if the inspector can access it. They are looking for a cracked cap, casing that sits too low to the ground, short cycling at the tank, and amperage draw that hints the pump is on its way out.

Water quality sampling is the third leg. The inspector pulls samples and sends them to a state certified lab. A basic loan panel usually covers total coliform and E. coli, nitrates, and sometimes lead. A fuller panel adds iron, manganese, hardness, pH, total dissolved solids, and arsenic where it is a known regional issue. The CDC and EPA recommend testing private wells at least annually for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH (https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/private-wells.html and https://www.epa.gov/privatewells), so a purchase inspection is really just your first annual test.

One caution on health topics: if any sample comes back positive for bacteria or high in nitrates, work through a state certified lab and your local health department on retesting and treatment. Do not rely on a home test strip to clear a well for drinking.

When do lenders require a well inspection?

Government backed loans are where most buyers first meet this requirement. FHA and VA loans commonly require well water testing, and often a distance check between the well and the septic system, before closing on a home with a private well. USDA rural loans usually ask for a water test too.

The details shift with handbook updates and local health authority rules, so treat this as commonly required and confirm the exact panel with your lender before you order anything. We have seen buyers pay twice because they ordered a generic test and the lender wanted samples pulled by a third party within 90 days of closing.

Conventional loans are looser. Many conventional lenders do not require a water test at all, but your inspector, your agent, and plain self interest all argue for one. You are buying the water supply along with the house.

Timing matters as well. Most lenders want test results dated within 60 to 90 days of closing. Order the inspection right after the purchase agreement is signed so a failed bacteria test leaves time for shock chlorination and a retest.

Is a combined well and septic inspection worth it?

Usually yes, if the property has both. A combined visit runs $600 to $1,000 or more, but booking the two separately often costs $150 to $300 extra once you pay two trip fees. The systems also interact. An inspector looking at both can check the separation distance between the wellhead and the septic field, which many health departments want at 50 to 100 feet.

A septic failure near a well is a contamination path, not just a plumbing bill. If the septic inspection turns up a failing drain field within that separation distance, insist on a fresh coliform and nitrate sample from the well before you close.

Quotes vary a lot by county; get three if the numbers you hear feel high. In some rural markets the local septic pumper does honest well checks for less than a franchise inspection outfit.

What drives the price up or down?

  - Depth and access: a wellhead buried in a pit or under a deck takes longer to inspect and costs more.

  - Lab panel size: a coliform and nitrate screen is cheap; adding arsenic, VOCs, or PFAS can add $100 to $400 in lab fees.

  - Travel: remote properties carry trip charges, sometimes $50 to $150.

  - Report requirements: lender ready reports with chain of custody documentation cost more than a verbal walkthrough.

  - Season: spring is peak season in many counties, and some inspectors book out two to three weeks.

One more variable is the well record itself. If the original drilling log is on file with the state, the inspector can confirm depth and casing without guesswork. Missing records mean more time on site.

How can you keep the well water inspection cost down?

The easiest saving is bundling. Book the well, septic, and general home inspection with outfits that coordinate, or with one company that does all three, and you cut trip fees. Ask each bidder what the reinspection fee is up front, because a failed bacteria test almost always means a second visit.

Negotiate who pays. In many rural markets the seller customarily covers the well and septic inspection, or splits it. Put it in the purchase agreement rather than assuming. If the seller had the well tested within the past year, ask for the lab sheet anyway, but pull your own sample too. Water conditions change, and last spring's clean coliform result says nothing about this spring.

Skip add ons you do not need yet. A PFAS panel is worth it in areas with known industrial contamination, but if your county health department has no advisories, you can defer that $250 to $400 test and start with the basics. Your local health office will tell you which contaminants show up in area wells, and that call is free.

Finally, check whether your county subsidizes testing. Plenty of health departments offer coliform and nitrate tests for $20 to $60 to encourage regular monitoring, which is a fraction of private lab pricing.

What happens after the inspection?

You get a written report covering yield, equipment condition, and lab results. Read the lab sheet yourself rather than skimming the summary. A total coliform hit is common in older wells and is often fixed with shock chlorination for $100 to $300, followed by a retest. High nitrates or arsenic are a different conversation and usually mean a treatment system.

If the results point to iron staining, rotten egg odor, or hardness, that is a treatment question rather than a safety emergency. Common questions

How much does a well inspection cost for an FHA loan?

Plan on $400 to $650 in most markets: a standard inspection plus the lab panel FHA appraisers commonly ask for, usually coliform, nitrate, nitrite, and lead. Requirements shift with handbook updates and local health authority rules, so confirm the exact panel and the sampling window with your lender before ordering.

Can I test well water myself instead of paying an inspector?

For your own peace of mind, yes. Mail in kits and direct lab drop offs cost $30 to $150 and are fine for annual monitoring. For a real estate transaction, most lenders require a disinterested third party to pull the sample, so a self collected test usually will not satisfy underwriting.

How long does a well inspection take?

Expect one to two hours on site for a standard inspection, longer if the flow test runs a full hour. Lab results take two to five business days for bacteria and nitrates. Build a week to ten days into your closing timeline, more if a failed test forces disinfection and a retest.

How often should a well be inspected after purchase?

Test the water every year at minimum. The CDC and EPA recommend annual testing of private wells for coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH. A full equipment inspection every three to five years is a reasonable rhythm, or sooner if pressure drops, water changes taste, or the pump starts short cycling.

Bottom line

Budget $250 to $400 for a standard well inspection, $400 to $650 with lab testing, and $600 to $1,000 or more for a full well and septic loan package. Order it early in your closing window, insist on a state certified lab, and keep the report. The $400 you spend now is cheap insurance against a $5,000 pump surprise or a bacteria problem you would otherwise drink.
