A well water inspection costs $150 to $900 in 2026, with most homeowners paying around $450. A basic visual inspection with flow-rate testing runs $150 to $300, while a comprehensive inspection with a full lab water panel runs $400 to $800. Where you land depends on scope, not just location.

Well depth, well type, and how many contaminants you test for all move the price. A shallow dug well with a simple bacteria and nitrate test costs far less than a deep drilled well getting a full metals-and-VOC panel plus a mechanical inspection of the pump and pressure system.

## What does a basic well inspection include?

A basic inspection is a visual check plus a flow-rate test, typically running $150 to $300. The inspector examines the well cap, casing, and visible components for damage, checks that the well is properly sealed, and measures how many gallons per minute the pump delivers under load.

This tier does not usually include a full laboratory water panel. If you want lab results included, confirm that up front, since "inspection" and "water testing" are priced and sold as separate line items by many companies.

## What does a comprehensive well inspection include?

A comprehensive inspection adds full laboratory testing to the visual and mechanical check, and typically runs $400 to $800 total. It covers everything in the basic tier plus a lab panel for bacteria, nitrates, pH, and total dissolved solids at minimum, often with additional contaminants based on regional risk factors.

Laboratory well water testing alone, without the mechanical inspection, runs $100 to $350 depending on how many parameters are tested. A state-certified lab is required for results to be reliable and, in many states, for results to be accepted by mortgage lenders or local health departments ([EPA, Protect Your Home's Water](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water)).

## What raises the cost of a well inspection?

| Cost factor | Typical impact |
|---|---|
| Well depth and type | Deeper drilled wells cost more to inspect than shallow dug wells |
| Number of contaminants tested | Each additional panel (VOCs, heavy metals, pesticides) adds lab cost |
| Travel distance | Some inspectors charge $1 to $2 per mile beyond a base service area |
| Bundling with septic inspection | Combined well and septic inspection: $400 to $650 |
| Bundling with home inspection | Combined well and home inspection: $400 to $830 |
| Real estate transaction timing | Rush inspections ahead of a closing date often carry a premium |

## Should you test for anything beyond the annual basics?

Both the EPA and CDC set the same annual floor for private wells: test every year for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH ([CDC, Guidelines for Testing Well Water](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/safety/guidelines-for-testing-well-water.html)). Beyond that floor, what you add depends on what's actually near your well, not a generic upsell panel.

The EPA publishes a direct table linking conditions to the tests they justify: stained plumbing points to iron, copper, or manganese; a nearby dry-cleaning operation or gas station points to volatile organic compounds; intensive agriculture nearby points to nitrates, pesticides, and coliform bacteria; and a metallic taste or corrosion points to pH and lead testing ([EPA, Protect Your Home's Water](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water)). Match your add-on tests to your actual situation instead of buying every panel a lab offers.

## When do you need a full inspection versus routine annual testing?

Routine annual testing is the coliform, nitrate, TDS, and pH panel you repeat every year regardless of anything changing. A full inspection is warranted on top of that when you're buying or selling a home with a private well, after flooding or nearby land disturbance, after repairing or replacing any part of the well system, or when your water's taste, color, or smell changes noticeably.

The CDC recommends testing immediately, not on the annual schedule, any time you notice a change in water quality or a new pregnancy or infant in the household, since those situations raise the stakes of a delayed result ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/safety/guidelines-for-testing-well-water.html)).

## FAQ

**How much does a well inspection cost before buying a house?**
Expect $400 to $830 if you bundle it with a general home inspection, or $250 to $550 for a well-only comprehensive inspection with lab testing. Real estate transactions typically require the comprehensive tier, not just a visual check.

**Is well water testing included in a home inspection?**
Not automatically. A general home inspector may note visible well components but usually doesn't collect water samples; well water lab testing is typically a separate line item or a specialist add-on.

**How often should a private well be inspected?**
Test annually for coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH at minimum, per CDC guidance. A full mechanical inspection is less frequent, typically at purchase, after well system repairs, or when water quality changes.

**What's the difference between a well inspection and a water test?**
An inspection covers the physical well, casing, cap, and pump mechanics. A water test analyzes a sample in a lab for specific contaminants. A comprehensive inspection package includes both; a basic one may include only the mechanical check.

**Why do well inspection quotes vary so much?**
Because "inspection" isn't a standardized service. A $150 quote is likely a visual and flow check only, while an $800 quote likely bundles a full lab panel. Ask exactly what's included before comparing prices.

Bottom line: a $150 to $300 basic check tells you the well itself is intact; the $400 to $800 comprehensive tier is what actually tells you whether the water is safe to drink. Match the tier to the reason you're testing, not the lowest quote you find.
