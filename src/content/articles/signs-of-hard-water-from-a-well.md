# Signs of Hard Water From a Well (and What to Do)

By the WellWater HQ team

The clearest signs of hard well water are soap and shampoo that won't lather well, a white or chalky scale buildup around faucets and showerheads, spotty dishes straight out of the dishwasher, and dry skin or dull hair after showering. Hardness comes from dissolved calcium and magnesium, and the U.S. Geological Survey classifies anything above 121 mg/L as hard, with water above 180 mg/L considered very hard ([USGS, Water Hardness](https://www.mgs.md.gov/groundwater/hardness.html), 2026).

Hard water isn't a health hazard. It's a nuisance and, over time, a cost problem, since scale buildup inside pipes, water heaters, and appliances reduces their efficiency and shortens their working life.

## What are the earliest signs of hard well water?

Soap behavior is usually the fastest tell. Hard water reacts with soap to form a sticky, insoluble curd instead of a smooth lather, which is why hard-water households often use noticeably more soap, shampoo, and dish detergent than a soft-water household to get the same clean feeling. That same reaction leaves a filmy residue on skin and hair, which is the actual cause of the "dry" or "squeaky" feeling a lot of people associate with hard water, not dryness from the water itself.

Spotting on glassware and dishes, even straight from a dishwasher on a supposedly clean cycle, shows up within the first few washes in genuinely hard water, since mineral deposits dry onto the surface as water evaporates.

## What signs take longer to show up?

Scale buildup is the slower, more expensive sign. White or chalky mineral deposits accumulate visibly around faucet aerators and showerheads over weeks to months, restricting flow gradually enough that a lot of homeowners don't notice until a showerhead is nearly clogged. Inside pipes and water heaters, that same scale builds invisibly, and a water heater running on hard water loses efficiency and heating capacity as scale insulates the heating element from the water it's supposed to be heating.

Laundry washed in hard water can look dingy or feel stiff over time, and dark clothing in particular tends to fade faster, both effects of mineral deposits building up in the fabric with every wash cycle rather than rinsing cleanly away.

## How hard is "hard," exactly?

Hardness is measured in milligrams per liter (mg/L) or grains per gallon (gpg), and one grain per gallon equals about 17.1 mg/L.

| Classification | mg/L (ppm) | Grains per gallon |
|---|---|---|
| Soft | 0 to 60 | 0 to 3.5 |
| Moderately hard | 61 to 120 | 3.5 to 7.0 |
| Hard | 121 to 180 | 7.0 to 10.5 |
| Very hard | Above 180 | Above 10.5 |

(USGS Water Hardness Classification, 2026)

## How do you confirm hard water for certain?

A home test strip gives a rough read, but a certified lab test as part of a broader well water panel gives an exact number in mg/L or gpg, which matters for sizing a softener correctly. Since private wells aren't covered by municipal water quality reports the way city water is, testing is the only way to know your actual hardness level rather than guessing from symptoms alone.

## What do you do about hard well water?

A water softener, sized to your household's actual hardness level and daily water use, is the standard fix. Ion-exchange softeners swap calcium and magnesium ions for sodium as water passes through resin, which eliminates the scale-forming minerals before they reach any fixture or appliance. Softener sizing (resin volume and regeneration frequency) depends directly on the gpg number from your test, along with household size, so an accurate test result matters more than a guess based on symptoms.

## FAQ

**What does hard well water do to skin and hair?**
It doesn't dry skin directly; the soap-and-mineral residue it leaves behind is what causes the tight, filmy feeling often described as "dry skin" after a shower, along with hair that feels dull or harder to rinse clean.

**Is hard well water safe to drink?**
Yes. Hardness is a cosmetic and nuisance issue, not a health hazard, and there's no EPA health-based limit on calcium or magnesium hardness in drinking water.

**How can I tell if I have hard water without a test?**
Watch for soap that won't lather well, white scale around faucets and showerheads, spotty dishes after washing, and reduced water pressure at fixtures over time as scale narrows pipe diameter. These are reliable indicators, but a lab test is the only way to get an exact number.

**Does a water softener remove all hardness minerals?**
A correctly sized softener removes essentially all calcium and magnesium hardness through ion exchange. Undersized softeners, a common installation mistake, only partially soften water and let some hardness through, especially during high-use periods.

**How often should well water be tested for hardness?**
Hardness doesn't shift as quickly as bacteria or nitrate levels, so testing every one to three years is generally sufficient unless symptoms noticeably worsen or a new well is being evaluated for the first time.

Bottom line: soap that won't lather and white scale around fixtures are the fastest signs of hard well water; scale inside appliances and water heaters is the slower, costlier one. Confirm the actual number with a lab test, above 121 mg/L is hard by USGS classification, and size a softener to that number rather than to guesswork.
