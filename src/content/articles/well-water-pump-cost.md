How much is a well water pump? The unit alone runs about $150 to $600 for a jet pump and $300 to $1,500 for a submersible, with constant pressure models reaching $2,500. Installed, most homeowners pay $1,000 to $2,800 for a full replacement, depending on depth, pump type, and local labor rates.

What type of well pump do you need?

The pump type is set mostly by your well depth, so start there. Your original well log, filed with the state in most cases, lists depth and the static water level. If you cannot find it, the county health department or state well database usually has a copy.

Shallow well jet pumps

A shallow well jet pump sits above ground, often in a basement or well house, and pulls water up by suction. Physics limits suction lift to roughly 25 feet, so these only work where the water level is high. They are the cheapest option, typically $150 to $400 for the unit, and the easiest to service because everything is at floor level.

Deep well jet pumps

A deep well jet pump also mounts above ground but pushes some water back down through a second pipe to an ejector in the well, which lets it lift from 25 to about 110 feet. Units run $300 to $600. They are less efficient than submersibles at the same depth, and we generally only recommend them where a well casing is too narrow for a submersible.

Submersible pumps

A submersible hangs down in the well below the water line and pushes water up, which is far more efficient than pulling it. This is the standard choice for wells deeper than 25 feet and works to 400 feet and beyond. A 1/2 hp unit for a typical home runs $300 to $800; 1 hp and 1.5 hp models for deep wells run $700 to $1,500. Because the pump lives a few hundred feet down, pulling it for replacement is where the labor bill comes from.

Constant pressure and variable speed pumps

Constant pressure systems pair a variable speed pump with an electronic controller so household pressure holds steady instead of swinging between the pressure switch settings. They are a real upgrade for homes with irrigation or multiple bathrooms, and they cost for it: $1,500 to $2,500 for equipment before labor.

Those parts add $100 to $400, and replacing them while the well is open is money well spent.

How do you size a pump by well depth and household demand?

Two numbers drive sizing: the depth to water and the flow your house needs. For flow, count fixtures. A common rule of thumb is one gpm per fixture, so a three bath house with a dishwasher, washer, and two hose bibs lands around 8 to 12 gpm. Irrigation changes the math fast; a sprinkler zone alone can demand 10 gpm.

For depth, the pump has to lift from the pumping level, not the static level, since the water drops while the pump runs. Horsepower follows from both numbers together. Very roughly: 1/2 hp handles most homes with water at 100 feet or less, 3/4 hp covers 100 to 250 feet, and 1 hp or more is deep well territory.

Do not oversize. A pump that outruns the well's recovery rate can pump the well dry and burn itself out running dry. If your well produces 5 gpm, a 12 gpm pump is a liability without a low water cutoff. This is one place where paying a licensed pump installer to do the sizing math beats guessing from a box label.

The pump also has to match your pressure tank. An undersized tank makes any pump short cycle, which kills motors early. We covered tank sizing in detail at WellWater HQ/well-water-pressure-tank, and it is worth reading before you sign a replacement quote, because a $300 tank added to the job can double the life of a $1,500 pump.

What else shows up on the invoice?

A few line items surprise people. Permits run $50 to $200 in counties that require them for pump work. Emergency and weekend calls carry premiums of $100 to $300, and pump failures have a talent for happening on holiday weekends. If the pressure switch, control box, or wiring from the panel is tired, electrical work can add $150 to $600.

Older wells sometimes need extras discovered mid job: a new well cap ($40 to $150), a torque arrestor, or in the worst case a camera inspection at $200 to $400 to figure out why the old pump seized. Ask every bidder how they handle surprises. A good contractor calls with a price before proceeding; a bad one hands you the total afterward.

None of this changes the core math much, but it explains why two honest quotes for the same pump can sit $700 apart. One includes the likely extras, and one bills them later.

What are the signs a well pump is failing?

  - Sputtering or air spitting at faucets, which can mean a failing pump, a drop pipe leak, or a well running low.

  - Pressure that swings or fades during showers, especially if it worsens over weeks.

  - The pump running constantly or clicking on and off every few seconds, called short cycling.

  - A jump in the electric bill with no other explanation; a struggling pump draws more current for less water.

  - Dirty, sandy, or cloudy water after years of clear water, which can mean the pump is sitting too low or the screen is failing.

  - Age: most submersibles last 10 to 15 years, jet pumps 8 to 12. Past that, budget for replacement even if it still runs.

Before you buy a pump, rule out the cheap fixes. A waterlogged pressure tank and a bad pressure switch each mimic pump failure, and both cost a fraction of a pump job. A switch is a $20 to $50 part. We have watched people replace a healthy $1,400 pump when the real problem was a $150 tank bladder.

If the water changed character along with the pressure, test before you treat. The CDC and EPA recommend testing private wells at least annually for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH (https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/private-wells.html and https://www.epa.gov/privatewells), and a pump swap is a natural moment to do it since the system is open anyway. For anything health related, use a state certified lab or your local health department, not a strip kit.

Repair or replace: which makes sense?

Above ground jet pumps are worth repairing. Motors, ejectors, and pressure switches are accessible and parts are cheap. Submersibles are a different story, because the labor to pull the pump is most of the bill. If a crew has already lifted 200 feet of drop pipe to diagnose a 12 year old pump, putting the old unit back rarely makes sense.

A decent rule: if the repair costs more than half of replacement and the pump is past two thirds of its expected life, replace it. And get itemized quotes. Quotes vary a lot by county; three bids on the same job can spread by $1,000, and the middle bid with a real parts list usually beats the cheap one with a lump sum.

Sediment and iron are quiet pump killers worth pricing into the decision. Grit wears impellers, and iron bacteria can clog screens years early. Common questions

How much is a well water pump for a 200 foot well?

Plan on a 3/4 hp to 1 hp submersible at $500 to $1,200 for the unit and $1,500 to $2,800 installed. Depth drives labor, since the crew pulls and resets 200 feet of drop pipe and wire. Add $100 to $400 if the drop pipe or splice kits need replacing while the well is open.

How long does a well pump last?

Submersibles typically run 10 to 15 years, jet pumps 8 to 12. Sediment, iron bacteria, low water levels, and short cycling from a bad pressure tank all shorten that. A correctly sized pump on a healthy tank, in clean water, can reach 20 years, but we would not budget on it.

Can I replace a well pump myself?

A shallow well jet pump swap is realistic for a handy owner, since it is plumbing and wiring at floor level. Pulling a submersible from a deep well is not a solo job: the pipe string can weigh hundreds of pounds, and a dropped pump can mean an unusable well. Many states also require licensed installers for work inside the casing.

Why did my new pump not fix my low pressure?

Usually because the pump was never the problem. A waterlogged pressure tank, a clogged sediment filter, scaled pipes, or a failing pressure switch all produce the same symptoms. Check the tank precharge and the switch first, and look at whole house filters for clogging, before blaming the new pump.

Bottom line

A well water pump costs $150 to $1,500 as a bare unit and roughly $1,000 to $2,800 installed for most homes, with constant pressure systems above that. Match the pump to your well depth and recovery rate, verify the pressure tank at the same time, and get three itemized bids. The sizing decision matters more than the brand on the box.
