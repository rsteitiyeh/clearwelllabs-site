By the WellWater HQ team

A positive total coliform test means bacteria got into your well or plumbing system somewhere, most often through a cracked casing, a loose well cap, or surface water intrusion, not necessarily that the water will make you sick. A positive E. coli or fecal coliform result is different: it points to fecal contamination and is treated as an immediate health risk requiring bottled or boiled water until the well is disinfected and retested clean.

Total coliforms are a broad group of bacteria found naturally in soil and plants; most aren't harmful on their own. The EPA uses them as an indicator organism, meaning their presence signals that something let contamination into a system that should be sealed, even if the coliform species itself is harmless.

## What's the difference between total coliform, fecal coliform, and E. coli?

Total coliform is the umbrella category: a group of related bacteria common in the environment, most of them not a direct health threat. Fecal coliform and E. coli are subsets of that group specifically associated with feces from warm-blooded animals, and their presence means a much more direct path from contamination to your tap.

Under the EPA's [Revised Total Coliform Rule](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/revised-total-coliform-rule-and-total-coliform-rule), any sample that tests positive for total coliform must also be analyzed for E. coli, because a total-coliform-only result and an E. coli-positive result carry different risk levels and require different responses (EPA, Revised Total Coliform Rule and Total Coliform Rule, 2026). Private wells aren't regulated the way public water systems are, but labs report results the same way, and the same distinction applies to what you do next.

## What should you do after a positive coliform test?

| Result | What it means | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| Total coliform positive, E. coli negative | Bacteria entered the system, likely surface intrusion or a sanitary defect | Inspect well cap and casing, shock chlorinate, retest in 7 to 10 days |
| Total coliform positive, E. coli or fecal coliform positive | Likely fecal contamination, direct health risk | Switch to bottled or boiled water immediately, shock chlorinate, retest, contact local health department |
| Repeat positive after disinfection | Ongoing contamination source or well defect | Have a licensed well contractor inspect the casing, seal, and grouting |

Boiling water at a rolling boil for at least one minute is the standard precaution while you sort out a positive result, per general CDC and state health department guidance for suspected bacterial contamination. Switch back to well water only after a clean retest from a certified lab.

## What causes coliform bacteria to get into a well?

Most contamination traces back to a physical entry point: a cracked or corroded well casing, a missing or damaged well cap, a pitless adapter that's failed, or surface water running toward the wellhead instead of away from it. Flooding, nearby land disturbance, and new construction near the well are the conditions the [EPA specifically flags as reasons to test immediately](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) rather than waiting for the annual cycle (EPA, Protect Your Home's Water, 2026).

Shallow, dug, or bored wells are more exposed than deep drilled wells with continuous casing, since they have less physical separation between the surface and the water table. A well that's tested clean for years can still turn positive after a heavy rain event if the casing has developed even a small defect.

## Does shock chlorination fix a coliform bacteria problem?

Usually, yes, for a total-coliform-positive result tied to a fixable entry point like a loose cap or a minor casing defect. Shock chlorination floods the well and plumbing with a high concentration of chlorine, holds it for at least 12 hours, then flushes it out, which kills the bacteria present at the time of treatment.

It does not fix the underlying entry point. If contamination keeps coming back after disinfection, the well itself, not the water in it, has a physical defect that needs a contractor's inspection, not another round of bleach.

## FAQ

**Is it safe to shower in water that tested positive for coliform bacteria?**
Showering is generally lower risk than drinking, since ingestion is the main exposure path, but avoid swallowing water and keep it away from open cuts until the well is disinfected and retested. If the result was E. coli or fecal coliform positive, switch to an alternate water source for all household uses until it clears.

**How long after shock chlorination should I retest for coliform?**
Wait at least 7 to 10 days after disinfection before sampling, since testing too early can be skewed by residual chlorine still in the system. Water sampling cannot proceed until all traces of chlorine have flushed out.

**Can a coliform-positive well be fixed permanently?**
Often, yes, if the entry point (a cracked casing, a bad seal, a damaged cap) gets repaired along with disinfection. If a well keeps testing positive after repeated treatment, a licensed well contractor needs to inspect the casing and grouting for a defect that disinfection alone can't fix.

**What's the difference between a "boil water" situation and a coliform-positive well?**
A boil water notice is typically issued for public systems; a positive coliform test on a private well is your own responsibility to act on. The recommended precaution, boiling at a rolling boil for at least one minute, is the same either way until the source is confirmed safe.

**Does a water softener or filter remove coliform bacteria?**
No. Standard softening and filtration equipment isn't certified for microbiological removal. Coliform contamination needs disinfection, either shock chlorination or a continuous UV system, not filtration.

Bottom line: a total-coliform-positive result means something let bacteria into your well and needs fixing, while an E. coli or fecal coliform positive means treat the water as unsafe right now. Either way, disinfect, retest through a certified lab, and don't rely on taste or clarity to tell you it's over.
