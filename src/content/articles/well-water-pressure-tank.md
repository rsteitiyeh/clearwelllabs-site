By the WellWater HQ team

A well pressure tank is sized by drawdown, the usable water it delivers between pump cycles, not by its gallon rating. The working rule is 1 gallon of drawdown per GPM of pump flow, and since only about a quarter of a tank's volume is usable drawdown, you multiply that number by roughly four to get the tank size you actually need.

A "52-gallon" tank does not give you 52 gallons of water between pump cycles. It gives you closer to 14 to 16 gallons, because the tank stores both water and compressed air, and only the water pushed out between the cut-in and cut-out pressure points counts as usable.

## What is drawdown, and why does it matter more than tank size?

Drawdown is the amount of water a pressure tank delivers before the pump has to switch back on, and it's what determines whether your system short-cycles. A tank sized only by gallon capacity, without checking drawdown against your pump's flow rate, can leave you with a pump that clicks on and off every time someone runs a faucet.

Three numbers set your drawdown: pump flow rate in GPM, the pressure switch's cut-in and cut-out settings, and tank size. Get any one of the three wrong and the tank underperforms regardless of how big it is on paper.

## How do you calculate the right tank size for your pump?

Use the 1:1 rule as your starting point: 1 gallon of drawdown for every 1 GPM of pump flow rate. A pump rated at 10 GPM needs at least 10 gallons of drawdown, which works out to roughly a 36-gallon tank once you account for the fact that only about a quarter of the tank's volume is usable water, per the sizing method published by the Water Systems Council, a nonprofit trade group representing the private well industry ([Water Systems Council, Sizing a Pressure Tank](https://www.watersystemscouncil.org/download/wellcare_information_sheets/basic_well_information_sheets/Sizing-a-Pressure-Tank.pdf)).

To find your pump's flow rate, run a garden hose at full blast into a 5-gallon bucket for 60 seconds with no other water running in the house; the gallons collected is your GPM. Higher pressure switch settings reduce drawdown for a given tank size, so a household running a 40/60 switch instead of 20/40 may need to step up a tank size to hit the same usable drawdown.

| Tank size | Approximate usable drawdown | Typical fit |
|---|---|---|
| 20 gallon | 5 to 6 gallons | Very small household, low-flow pump |
| 44 gallon | 11 to 13 gallons | Common residential minimum |
| 85 gallon | 21 to 25 gallons | Larger households, 10 to 15 GPM pumps |
| 120 gallon | 30+ gallons | High-demand homes, irrigation systems |

## What are standard cut-in and cut-out pressure switch settings?

The three standard settings are 20/40, 30/50, and 40/60 PSI, where the first number is cut-in, the pressure at which the pump turns on, and the second is cut-out, the pressure at which it shuts off. Most modern homes run 40/60, per Pentair's own pressure switch adjustment instructions for its Fleck and residential well equipment line ([Pentair, Adjusting Well Pump Pressure Switches](https://www.pentair.com/content/dam/extranet/web/nam/parts2o/manuals/adjusting-well-pump-pressure-switches-instructions.pdf)).

Mismatching the switch setting to the tank's pre-charge, or mixing components rated for different ranges, is a common cause of short-cycling, where the pump turns on and off far more often than it should and wears out prematurely.

## How do you set the tank's pre-charge pressure?

Set the tank's air pre-charge 2 PSI below the cut-in setting for lower-pressure systems: a 30/50 switch needs 28 PSI in the tank. For higher-pressure 40/60 systems, that same 2 PSI margin isn't enough; a better guideline is 10 to 15 percent below cut-in, which works out to roughly 34 to 36 PSI rather than 38.

You must drain all water from the tank before checking or adjusting the air charge, since a pressurized tank will give a false reading. Check it with a tire pressure gauge, ideally a digital one, and add air with a bicycle pump or small air compressor through the tank's air valve.

## What does a pressure tank cost installed?

Replacing a well pressure tank costs $800 to $1,500 including the tank and professional labor, with installation labor alone running $200 to $500 for a straightforward one-to-two-hour swap. Equipment cost varies a lot by build quality: hardware-store steel bladder tanks run $150 to $500 but typically last only 5 to 7 years before the bladder fails, while pro-grade steel tanks like Well-X-Trol run $1,095 to $2,295 and are built for 15-plus years of service.

## How long do pressure tanks last, and what shortens their life?

A quality bladder or diaphragm tank lasts 15 or more years when properly pre-charged and matched to a correctly set pressure switch. A bargain tank with a failing bladder, or any tank running with the wrong pre-charge, tends to fail closer to the 5 to 7 year mark.

Short-cycling is the main life-shortener: every extra pump start beyond what a correctly sized, correctly charged tank would need adds wear to both the pump and the pressure switch, not just the tank itself.

## FAQ

**What size pressure tank do I need for a well pump?**
Use the 1:1 rule: 1 gallon of drawdown per GPM of pump flow, then size the tank so roughly a quarter of its total volume covers that drawdown. A 10 GPM pump typically needs a tank in the 36-gallon range or larger.

**How do I check my well pressure tank's air charge?**
Drain the tank completely first, then check the air valve with a tire pressure gauge. The reading should sit 2 PSI below cut-in for a 30/50 switch, or roughly 10 to 15 percent below cut-in for a 40/60 switch.

**Why does my well pump short-cycle?**
Usually a mismatch between the tank's pre-charge, the pressure switch setting, and the tank's drawdown capacity. Re-checking all three together, not just replacing one part, usually resolves it.

**How often should a well pressure tank be replaced?**
Budget tanks with a failed bladder often need replacement around 5 to 7 years; pro-grade steel tanks with proper pre-charge maintenance commonly run 15 or more years.

**What pressure switch setting is standard for a home well system?**
40/60 PSI is the most common in modern homes, though 20/40 and 30/50 are also standard depending on the system's design and the plumbing fixtures it serves.

Bottom line: size by drawdown, not gallon rating, and get the pre-charge within a few PSI of correct for your switch setting. Those two numbers, done right, are what keep a pump from short-cycling and a tank from failing early.
