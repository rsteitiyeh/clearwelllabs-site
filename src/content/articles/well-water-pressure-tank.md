Well water system pressure tank: sizing and setup

A well water system pressure tank stores water under air pressure so your pump does not start every time someone opens a tap. Size it so drawdown is at least one gallon per gpm of pump flow, ideally 1.5 to 2, and set the air precharge 2 psi below the pressure switch cut in setting.

What does the pressure tank actually do?

The tank does two jobs. First, it stores a few gallons of pressurized water so small draws, a toilet flush or a glass of water, come from the tank instead of waking the pump. Second, and more important, it protects the pump from short cycling, the rapid on and off starts that overheat the motor and burn contacts in the pressure switch.

Inside a modern tank, a butyl bladder or diaphragm separates the water from a cushion of pressurized air. As the pump fills the tank, the air compresses. When you open a faucet, that compressed air pushes water back out until the system pressure falls to the switch's cut in point and the pump starts again. Older galvanized tanks skip the bladder and let air sit directly on the water, which works until the air slowly dissolves away.

Pump motors are hardest on themselves at startup. Each start draws several times running current and flexes the motor windings. Manufacturers commonly want starts held to around 100 or fewer per day, and the tank is the only thing standing between your pump and hundreds of starts. That is why a $300 tank decision affects a $1,500 pump's lifespan; we priced that relationship out in our pump cost guide at WellWater HQ/well-water-pump-cost.

How do you size a well water system pressure tank?

The number that matters is drawdown, the usable water the tank actually delivers between pump starts, not the sticker size. A 44 gallon tank at a 40/60 switch setting delivers only about 12 gallons of drawdown, because the rest of the volume is air cushion and dead water.

The standard rule ties drawdown to pump flow and minimum run time. You want the pump to run at least one minute per cycle, ideally closer to two. So a 10 gpm pump needs at least 10 gallons of drawdown, and 15 to 20 is more comfortable. Here is how that maps to nominal tank sizes at a 40/60 setting. Figures are approximate and vary a little by brand.

|                    |                             |                               |
| ------------------ | --------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| **Pump flow rate** | **Minimum drawdown needed** | **Typical nominal tank size** |
| Up to 5 gpm        | 5 gallons                   | 20 gallon tank                |
| 5 to 8 gpm         | 8 gallons                   | 32 gallon tank                |
| 8 to 12 gpm        | 12 gallons                  | 44 gallon tank                |
| 12 to 17 gpm       | 17 gallons                  | 62 gallon tank                |
| 17 to 24 gpm       | 24 gallons                  | 86 gallon tank                |
| 24 gpm and up      | 33 gallons or more          | 119 gallon tank or two tanks  |

When in doubt, size up. A bigger tank costs $50 to $150 more at each step, cycles the pump less, and gives you more stored water during a power blip. The only real downsides are floor space and a slightly wider pressure swing between showers. We have never heard a well owner complain the tank was too big.

Expect to pay roughly $150 to $400 for a quality 20 to 44 gallon diaphragm tank, $400 to $800 for the big sizes, and $200 to $500 in labor for a swap. Quotes vary a lot by county; get three if the first number seems steep.

What should the precharge be set to?

Set the air precharge 2 psi below the pressure switch cut in. On a common 40/60 switch, that means 38 psi. On a 30/50 switch, 28 psi. The 2 psi gap makes sure a sip of water remains in the tank at cut in, so the bladder is not slammed empty and the switch sees a smooth pressure curve.

Checking it takes ten minutes twice a year:

1.  Kill power to the pump at the breaker.

2.  Open a faucet and drain the system until pressure reads zero.

3.  Check the air valve on top of the tank with a tire gauge.

4.  Add or bleed air to hit 2 psi below cut in, then restore power.

A tank that reads low usually just needs air. A tank that will not hold air, or that spits water from the air valve, has a failed bladder and is done. Precharge only reads true with the water side drained; checking it against a pressurized system tells you nothing.

How do you fix a waterlogged pressure tank?

Waterlogged means the air cushion is gone and the tank is nearly full of water. With no air spring, pressure crashes the moment a tap opens and the pump short cycles, sometimes several times a minute. You can hear it: rapid clicking at the switch and a pump that never rests.

The quick diagnosis is the knuckle test. Rap the tank top to bottom. A healthy tank sounds hollow up top and dull at the bottom; a waterlogged one sounds dull nearly all the way up. The tank will also feel heavy and may rock less when pushed.

Here is the step by step we use:

5.  Confirm short cycling: watch the pressure gauge while a faucet runs. Swings from cut in to cut out in under 30 seconds point to the tank.

6.  Kill pump power and drain the tank completely through a hose bib or faucet.

7.  Check precharge at the air valve. Depress the pin briefly: if water comes out, the bladder is ruptured and the tank needs replacement.

8.  If only air comes out, pump the precharge up to 2 psi below cut in with a compressor or bike pump.

9.  Restore power, let the system pressurize, and watch a few cycles. Run time should now exceed one minute.

10. Recheck the precharge in a week. If it dropped again, the bladder or the air valve core is leaking and replacement is the fix.

On an old galvanized tank without a bladder, waterlogging is routine rather than fatal; the air simply dissolves into the water over months. Recharging it with air is normal maintenance, though most owners eventually replace these with diaphragm tanks to end the ritual.

Where should the tank live, and what does a swap involve?

Put the tank close to where the water line enters the house, upstream of any treatment gear, on a dry level floor where a leak will not ruin anything. Basements, utility rooms, and insulated well houses all work. What does not work is a spot that freezes; a frozen tank tee or gauge line is a common winter no water call in cold states.

A straight swap is a half day job for a handy owner: kill the pump, drain down, unthread the tank tee, set the new tank, and rebuild the tee with a new gauge, relief valve, and switch while everything is apart. Those parts cost $40 to $80 total and are almost always worth replacing at the same time, since a ten year old switch on a new tank is the weak link.

Two cautions. Support big tanks properly, because a full 86 gallon tank weighs several hundred pounds. And never plug a bladder tank's air valve leak with a cap; that valve is your only window into precharge health.

How does the tank fit the rest of the system?

The tank works as a team with the pressure switch and any treatment equipment downstream. Common questions

How long does a well pressure tank last?

Diaphragm and bladder tanks typically last 8 to 15 years. Sediment, iron bacteria, and chronic short cycling shorten that; correct precharge and clean water stretch it. Cheap builder grade tanks sit at the low end. Once a bladder fails there is no repair, only replacement, so budget around $500 to $900 installed when yours passes year ten.

Can a pressure tank be too big?

Practically, no. A larger tank means longer pump run times and fewer starts, which motors prefer. The trade offs are price, floor space, and longer waits between cut in and cut out, so pressure wanders across a wider band. The one caution is chlorinated or treated water sitting too long in an enormous tank on a tiny household draw.

Why does my pump click on and off rapidly?

Short cycling is almost always a lost air charge or a ruptured bladder in the pressure tank. Check precharge first with the system drained. Less common causes: a clogged or waterlogged switch nipple, a stuck check valve, or a leak between the tank and the well. Fix it quickly, because every start eats pump life.

What pressure should a 40/60 switch system show at the tap?

You will see pressure drift between 40 and 60 psi as the tank empties and refills; that swing is normal. If it drops below 40 before the pump kicks in, or the gauge slams from 60 to 40 in seconds, check the tank precharge. For steadier pressure, some owners move to a constant pressure pump system instead.

Bottom line

Size your well water system pressure tank so drawdown at least matches pump gpm, set the precharge 2 psi under cut in, and knuckle test the tank twice a year. A correctly sized and charged tank is the cheapest pump insurance you can buy: roughly $300 in steel and butyl protecting $1,500 or more of pump down the well.
