# Well Water Testing Kits: What They Actually Test

By the WellWater HQ team

A well water testing kit is usually a set of chemically treated strips that change color for pH, hardness, chlorine, iron, and a handful of other aesthetic markers, read against a color chart in under a minute. What most kits do not reliably test is the two things that matter most for health: total coliform bacteria and nitrate, both of which need lab-grade methods to produce a number you can trust.

That gap is why the EPA and most state health departments recommend a certified laboratory for the annual test, and treat drugstore kits as a rough first screen at best. The two aren't interchangeable, and knowing which one you're holding changes what you should actually do with the result.

## What does a basic well water test strip actually detect?

Strip kits typically read pH, total hardness, total chlorine, alkalinity, iron, copper, lead, nitrate, and nitrite, using a colorimetric reaction you compare against a printed chart. Some kits fold in a coliform test using a self-culture vial that changes color if bacteria grow over 48 hours, which is a real test, just a slower and less sensitive one than a lab culture.

The strip-based results for pH, hardness, and chlorine are generally close enough for a first look, since those are simple chemical reactions with wide acceptable ranges. Iron and nitrate strips are less reliable, because they rely on matching a color gradient by eye, and the difference between "borderline" and "over the limit" on a nitrate strip can be genuinely hard to call.

## What does a certified lab test for that a strip kit can't?

A certified laboratory runs total coliform and E. coli through a regulated culture method with a defined result (present or absent), not a color match. The same lab reports nitrate and nitrite as a precise number in milligrams per liter, run on calibrated equipment, rather than a shade between two printed swatches.

Labs also test for contaminants no strip kit covers at all: arsenic, radon, uranium, PFAS, and volatile organic compounds, depending on the panel you order. The [EPA recommends every private well owner test annually](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) for total coliform bacteria, nitrate, total dissolved solids, and pH through a state-certified lab, and every three to five years for a broader panel covering metals and other contaminants (EPA, Protect Your Home's Water, 2026).

## Strip kit vs. certified lab: what each one actually covers

| Parameter | Home strip kit | Certified lab | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| pH | Yes, reliable | Yes | Affects corrosion and treatment choice |
| Hardness | Yes, reliable | Yes | Determines softener need |
| Total chlorine | Yes, reliable | Yes | Relevant on shared or chlorinated wells |
| Iron | Yes, rough estimate | Yes, precise | Staining threshold is exact (0.3 mg/L) |
| Nitrate | Yes, rough estimate | Yes, precise | Health limit is exact (10 mg/L) |
| Total coliform / E. coli | Sometimes, slower, less sensitive | Yes, regulated method | Direct health risk |
| Arsenic, radon, PFAS, VOCs | No | Yes (add-on panels) | Not detectable without lab equipment |

## How accurate are DIY well water testing kits?

Accurate enough to flag an obvious problem, not accurate enough to rule one out. A strip that reads clean for nitrate at 8 mg/L and one that reads clean at 2 mg/L can look identical on a color chart, even though one is close to the EPA's 10 mg/L health limit and the other isn't. That margin matters most for the contaminants tied to acute health risk, which is exactly where strip kits are weakest.

We'd treat a strip kit result the way you'd treat a home glucose test: useful for a quick check between real ones, not a substitute for the lab work when the answer actually matters, like before giving well water to an infant or after a flood.

## How often should you test, and with which kind?

Test annually with a certified lab for total coliform, nitrate, TDS, and pH, per EPA guidance (EPA, Protect Your Home's Water, 2026), and run the broader metals and VOC panel every three to five years or after any change in taste, color, or odor. Use a strip kit in between for a quick sanity check, or right after a plumbing repair, but don't let a clean strip result talk you out of the annual certified test.

Retest immediately, strip or lab, if you notice a change in taste, color, or smell, if there's been flooding or nearby land disturbance, or if anyone in the house is pregnant, nursing, or bottle-feeding an infant.

## FAQ

**Are well water test strips accurate enough to trust?**
For pH, hardness, and chlorine, generally yes. For nitrate, iron, and bacteria, treat a strip result as a rough screen only. Confirm anything borderline or concerning with a certified laboratory test.

**Can a home test kit detect bacteria in well water?**
Some kits include a self-culture bacteria vial that changes color after 48 hours if coliform bacteria are present. It's a real test, but less sensitive than a certified lab culture, and it won't distinguish total coliform from E. coli the way a lab panel does.

**How much does a certified well water lab test cost?**
A basic panel covering coliform bacteria, nitrate, and a few other parameters typically runs $20 to $150 depending on the lab and state; broader panels covering metals, VOCs, or PFAS cost more. Contact your local health department first, since some offer free or subsidized well testing.

**What should a new well water testing kit include at minimum?**
At minimum, look for pH, hardness, iron, and nitrate strips, plus a coliform bacteria test. That covers the aesthetic basics, but it still isn't a substitute for the EPA-recommended annual certified lab test.

**Do I need to test well water if it looks and tastes fine?**
Yes. Nitrate, arsenic, and bacteria are all undetectable by taste, smell, or appearance. Clear, good-tasting water can still fail a lab test, which is the entire reason annual testing exists.

Bottom line: a strip kit is a fast way to catch an obvious problem between real tests, not a replacement for the certified lab work the EPA recommends every year. Use both, but trust the lab for anything that could actually affect your family's health.
