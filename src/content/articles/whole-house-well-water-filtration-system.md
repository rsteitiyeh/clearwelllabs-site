# Whole House Well Water Filtration System: Sizing and Cost

By the WellWater HQ team

A whole house well water filtration system treats water at the point it enters your home, before it reaches any fixture, so every tap, shower, and appliance gets the same treated water. Sizing runs on your peak flow rate in gallons per minute; cost runs on how many contaminants your lab report actually flags, from about $500 for a single sediment stage to $6,500 or more for a full multi-stage setup with iron, carbon, and disinfection.

Whole house treatment is different from a single under-sink or pitcher filter, which only treats water at one tap and does nothing for the shower, washing machine, or water heater. A well that tests positive for iron staining or sediment grit needs the point-of-entry version; a household that just wants better-tasting drinking water at the kitchen sink can often get away with a single point-of-use filter instead.

## What's the difference between whole-house and point-of-use filtration?

Whole-house (point-of-entry) systems tie into the main line after the pressure tank, so treated water reaches every fixture in the house. Point-of-use systems, like an under-sink cartridge or a pitcher filter, treat water at a single tap and leave the rest of the plumbing untouched.

Sediment, iron, manganese, and taste-and-odor problems generally call for whole-house treatment, since they affect laundry, dishes, and plumbing fixtures as much as drinking water. Contaminants that only matter for water you actually drink or cook with, like nitrate or arsenic, are often handled more cheaply with a point-of-use reverse osmosis unit at the kitchen sink rather than treating the whole house.

## How do you size a whole house filtration system to your home's peak flow?

Size to the highest number of gallons per minute your house is likely to pull at once, not the average. Two fixtures running together, such as a shower and a washing machine, is a realistic peak for most households, not an edge case.

Here's that worked through for a real household: a 3-bathroom home with a showerhead rated at 2.5 GPM, a washing machine drawing about 3 GPM, and a kitchen faucet at 2 GPM running at the same time adds up to roughly 7.5 GPM of simultaneous demand. Installers typically add a margin on top of that figure, which is why most 3-bathroom homes end up sized to a 10 to 12 GPM system rather than the bare 7.5 GPM peak. A 2-bathroom home usually lands at 7 to 10 GPM; a 4-bathroom home can require 12 to 15 GPM or more. Undersizing shows up immediately as a pressure drop the moment two fixtures run together.

## What does a whole house filtration system cost by treatment tier?

| Treatment tier | What it covers | Typical installed cost |
|---|---|---|
| Sediment only | Grit, sand, cloudiness (spin-down or cartridge) | $500 to $1,500 |
| Two-stage | Sediment plus iron or carbon | $1,500 to $3,500 |
| Full multi-stage | Sediment, iron/manganese, carbon, and UV or RO | $3,500 to $6,500+ |

Most well owners with typical water quality, meaning some sediment and iron but no bacteria, land in the $1,500 to $5,000 range for a properly sequenced multi-stage system, according to [2026 contractor pricing from HomeGuide](https://homeguide.com/costs/well-water-filtration-system-cost) (HomeGuide, Well Water Filtration System Cost, 2026). Adding whole-house UV disinfection or reverse osmosis pushes a full setup toward the top of that range or past it. Budget $100 to $300 a year afterward for filter and media replacement on top of the installed price (HomeGuide, 2026), since that ongoing cost is the part most homeowners forget to plan for.

## Where does a whole-house filter get installed, and in what order?

Installation happens on the main line after the pressure tank and before the water heater, so every downstream fixture gets treated water. Sequencing matters: sediment filtration goes first to protect everything after it, iron and manganese removal comes next, and carbon filtration for taste and odor goes last, right before the water reaches the house.

Getting the order wrong is one of the most common retrofit mistakes. Installing carbon before iron removal, for example, fouls the carbon media with iron sediment and shortens its working life well below what the cartridge is rated for. A licensed installer typically completes a full multi-stage install in a single day, with bypass valves on each stage so you can service one unit without shutting off water to the whole house.

## How long do the components last, and what does upkeep look like?

Sediment cartridges need changing every one to three months, more often after storms or seasonal water table shifts that stir up grit. Iron filter media typically lasts several years before replacement, and carbon cartridges generally need swapping every six to twelve months. A UV lamp, if included, needs an annual bulb change regardless of whether it looks like it's still working.

None of that replaces annual water testing. The [EPA recommends every private well owner test at least once a year](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) for total coliform bacteria, nitrate, total dissolved solids, and pH (EPA, Protect Your Home's Water, 2026). A filtration system treats what your last test told you was there; it doesn't tell you what's changed since.

## FAQ

**How many GPM do I need for a whole house well water filtration system?**
Most 2-bathroom homes need 7 to 10 GPM; 3 and 4-bathroom homes typically need 10 to 15 GPM or more. Size to your realistic peak, meaning two or three fixtures running together, not your home's average daily use.

**Does a whole house filter remove bacteria from well water?**
No. Standard sediment, iron, and carbon filtration isn't certified for microbiological removal. A positive coliform test needs UV disinfection or shock chlorination, not filtration alone.

**Is a whole house filtration system worth it over a single under-sink filter?**
If your test shows sediment, iron, manganese, or taste and odor problems, yes, since those affect every fixture in the house, not just the kitchen tap. If your only concern is drinking water taste, a point-of-use filter is cheaper and often enough.

**How much does a whole house well water filtration system cost installed?**
Most homeowners spend $1,500 to $5,000 for a properly sequenced multi-stage system, with basic sediment-only setups starting around $500 and full multi-stage systems with UV or RO running $3,500 to $6,500 or more.

**Do I need a new system if I already have a water softener?**
Not necessarily. A softener treats hardness through ion exchange; it doesn't filter sediment, iron, or organics. Most well houses with hard, iron-bearing water run a softener and a filtration system in sequence rather than one replacing the other.

Bottom line: size to your realistic peak GPM, not your average use, and match the treatment tier to what your lab report actually shows instead of a generic big-box package. A system built around your own numbers costs more upfront in research and less over the life of the equipment.
