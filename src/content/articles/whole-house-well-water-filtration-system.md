A whole house well water filtration system treats every drop at the point of entry, sized to your peak flow in gallons per minute. Size to the house, not the well pump: two bathrooms need roughly 7 to 10 gpm of service flow. Installed costs run about $300 for sediment-only up to $8,000 for a full treatment train, approximate.

What makes a whole house system different from a single filter?

Point-of-use filters, like an under-sink unit, protect one tap. A whole house well water filtration system installs where the water line enters the home, right after the pressure tank, so showers, laundry, the water heater, and every faucet get treated water. That distinction drives both the benefits and the sizing math.

The benefit side is mostly about the stuff you own. Iron stains tubs and laundry. Hardness scales water heaters into early graves. Sediment chews up fixture cartridges and appliance valves. Treat at the point of entry and all of that stops at once.

The cost side is flow. A kitchen tap needs half a gallon a minute. A whole house at peak demand, two showers plus a laundry load, can pull 10 to 14 gpm. Every tank and cartridge in the train has to pass that flow without choking pressure, and that is where most bad installs go wrong.

How do you size a whole house system to household flow rate?

Sizing has one goal: the treatment train must handle your realistic peak demand without dropping below about 40 psi at the fixtures. Two numbers matter, and only one of them is on the equipment brochure.

Step 1: estimate your peak service flow

Count fixtures that plausibly run at the same time. A shower draws about 2 to 2.5 gpm, a faucet 1 to 1.5, a washing machine 2 to 3, a dishwasher 1.5. A weekday morning in a two-bath house with laundry running lands around 7 to 10 gpm. Bigger homes with body-spray showers or irrigation tie-ins can hit 15 to 20 gpm.

A quick shortcut many installers use: bathrooms times 4 gpm, plus 2. It overshoots slightly, which is the correct direction to miss in. Undersized equipment is the single most common defect we find in existing installs.

Step 2: check what the well can actually deliver

Your treatment train cannot flow more than the pump and pressure tank supply. Check the pump's rated gpm and the tank's drawdown before buying anything; a 7 gpm pump feeding a 12 gpm treatment design just moves the bottleneck. If the pump is marginal or the tank short cycles, deal with that first. We keep current replacement numbers at WellWater HQ/well-water-pump-cost and tank sizing basics at WellWater HQ/well-water-pressure-tank.

Backwashing filters add a wrinkle: an air-injection iron filter may need 5 to 8 gpm of backwash flow to clean its own media bed. If the well cannot supply that, the media never fully regenerates and iron starts leaking through months later. Ask every installer what backwash flow their proposed tank needs. If they do not know, keep shopping.

Step 3: match port and cartridge sizes to the flow

Filter housings and valves are the pipe fittings of the treatment world, and small ones throttle the whole house. Here is the sizing table we sketch on graph paper for readers, approximate and worth confirming against manufacturer flow curves.

|               |                        |                                                        |
| ------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Bathrooms** | **Service flow (gpm)** | **Recommended port / cartridge size (approx.)**        |
| 1             | 5 to 7                 | 3/4 in. ports; 10 in. standard cartridges              |
| 2             | 7 to 10                | 1 in. ports; 10 in. Big Blue (4.5 in.) cartridges      |
| 3             | 10 to 14               | 1 in. ports; 20 in. Big Blue cartridges or 10x54 tanks |
| 4             | 12 to 16               | 1 to 1.25 in. ports; 20 in. Big Blue or 12x52 tanks    |
| 5+            | 16 to 25               | 1.5 in. ports; parallel housings or commercial valves  |

The pattern to internalize: pressure drop rises steeply with flow. A 10 inch standard cartridge that is fine at 4 gpm becomes a 15 psi restriction at 10 gpm once it loads up with sediment. Oversizing housings costs $40 extra up front and pays you back every single shower.

What is the correct stage order for whole house treatment?

Order is not a style choice. Each stage protects the next, and swapping two of them can quietly destroy thousands of dollars of media. The correct point-of-entry sequence, using only the stages your lab test justifies:

  - Pressure tank first, always. Filters need steady pressure, not raw pump surges.

  - Sediment filter next, so grit never reaches valves and media beds.

  - Acid neutralizer here if pH is under 6.5, before water touches more metal.

  - Iron and sulfur filter before the softener. Iron ruins softener resin permanently.

  - Water softener after iron removal, sized in grains to household use.

  - Carbon filtration after the softener (and after any chlorine contact tank, so it strips the residual).

  - UV disinfection dead last, because the lamp only works on clear water.

Reverse osmosis stays out of the whole-house train for most homes. It wastes reject water and costs too much at 10 gpm scale; a point-of-use RO at the kitchen sink covers drinking water for a fraction of the price. Stage-by-stage detail on each of these lives in the main pillar at WellWater HQ/well-water-filtration-system.

The single most common order mistake we encounter is softener-before-iron-filter, usually because the softener came first historically and the iron filter was bolted on upstream of nothing. If your resin is already iron fouled, resin cleaners help mildly, but plan on early replacement.

A worked sizing example, start to finish

Numbers stick better with a real house attached, so here is one we sized last spring for a reader in Michigan. Three bathrooms, four people, lab report showing 2.1 mg/L iron, 14 grains hardness, faint sulfur smell, bacteria negative, pH 7.2.

Peak flow first: three bathrooms times 4 gpm plus 2 gives 14 gpm design flow. The well pump was rated 12 gpm with a healthy pressure tank, so 12 gpm became the practical ceiling, still comfortably above the realistic morning peak of about 10.

Configuration next: the iron level and smell called for an air-injection iron and sulfur filter in a 10x54 tank rated 12 gpm service and 7 gpm backwash, which the pump could supply. Hardness at 14 grains justified a 48,000 grain softener downstream. A 20 inch Big Blue sediment housing with 1 inch ports went in front of everything. No UV, because bacteria tested negative two years running, with the caveat to retest annually.

Final tally: about $4,100 installed by a local plumber, at the lower end of full-train pricing because no UV or neutralizer was needed. The reader's own three quotes ranged from $3,900 to $5,600 for equivalent equipment, which is why we keep repeating the get-three-quotes line.

The takeaway is the method, not the model numbers. Flow ceiling from the pump, design flow from the bathroom count, stages from the lab report, sizes from the flow. Run those four steps in that order and it is hard to buy badly.

Where the equipment goes, and climate details

Whole house trains want a spot right after the pressure tank: basement wall, utility room, or well house. Leave 12 inches of clearance above tanks for media loading, a floor drain or utility sink within hose reach for backwash discharge, and an outlet for valve heads and UV.

In cold climates, never put treatment equipment anywhere that freezes. A frozen cartridge housing splits, and a split housing on the pressure side of the plumbing floods until someone notices. Insulated well houses need heat tape or a lamp on a thermostat; verify it before the first hard freeze, not after.

Garages in mild climates work, with one caution: backwash cycles run at 2 a.m. by default and they are not silent. Program regeneration times, and put the train on the far wall from bedrooms if you can. Small thing, but it is the kind of detail that decides whether the household likes the system.

Test first, buy second

None of the configurations above should be picked from symptoms alone. The EPA and CDC recommend testing private wells at least annually for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH (https://www.epa.gov/privatewells and https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/private-wells.html). Annual matters because wells change; a clean result three years ago says little about today.

Use a state-certified lab for anything you will spend money on, and contact your local health department if bacteria or nitrates show up. They know the local geology and the local failure patterns better than any national article can. DIY install or hire a plumber?

Cartridge housings are honest DIY territory: cut the line, add a bypass, mount the housing, done in an afternoon with $30 of fittings. Backwashing tanks are a bigger lift. They need a drain within reach, correct valve programming, sometimes a 120V outlet, and enough clearance to service. Most homeowners hire those out at $300 to $800 of labor per tank.

Two non-negotiables regardless of who installs: a three-valve bypass around the whole train, so a failed filter never leaves the house dry, and a sample tap between major stages, so you can test where a problem starts instead of guessing. Both are cheap during install and annoying to retrofit.

And keep a folder. Valve settings, media install dates, lab reports, and receipts. When you sell the house, that folder is proof the well was cared for, and buyers' inspectors notice.

Common questions

What size whole house filter do I need for a 3 bathroom house?

Plan around 10 to 14 gpm of service flow. That means 1 inch ports minimum, 20 inch Big Blue cartridges for sediment, and backwashing tanks in the 10x54 size class. Confirm against the manufacturer's flow curve at your target pressure drop, not the peak flow number printed on the box, which assumes a pressure loss you would notice in the shower.

How much does a whole house well water filtration system cost?

Approximate installed ranges: sediment only, $300 to $900. Sediment plus carbon, $800 to $2,000. Iron package, $1,500 to $3,200. Full train with softener and UV, $3,500 to $8,000. Add $150 to $400 per year for cartridges, salt, and lamps. DIY installs save roughly 40 to 60 percent. Quotes vary a lot by county; get three.

Does a whole house system replace an under-sink RO?

No, they do different jobs. Whole house stages remove sediment, iron, hardness, odor, and bacteria for every tap. RO removes dissolved solids like nitrates and arsenic that those stages pass through. Many well owners run both: whole house treatment at the point of entry and a small RO at the kitchen tap for drinking water.

Will a whole house filter lower my water pressure?

A correctly sized system costs you 3 to 5 psi across the train, which most people never notice. Undersized housings or clogged cartridges are what crater pressure. If your shower weakens over weeks and recovers after a cartridge change, the filter was doing its job and telling you the service interval. If it is weak from day one, the sizing is wrong.

How long does a whole house system last?

Housings and valves commonly run 10 to 20 years. Media has its own clock: softener resin 10 to 15 years without iron fouling, iron filter media 5 to 10, carbon 3 to 6 in tank form, UV lamps 1 year. Budget for media replacement at those intervals rather than whole-system replacement, and the hardware itself is a once-a-generation buy.

Bottom line

Size a whole house well water filtration system to peak household flow, roughly 4 gpm per bathroom plus 2, keep the stage order sediment, iron, softener, carbon, UV, and buy only the stages your state-certified lab test justifies. For most two-to-three-bath homes that lands between $1,500 and $4,000 installed, approximate, and protects every appliance the water touches.
